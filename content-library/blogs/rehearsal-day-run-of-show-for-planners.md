Rehearsal day is the last window where the run-of-show can still be tested, edited, and signed off before an audience arrives. Every department has a list. Production has finite hours. The planner's job is not to run the show — it's to protect the blocks on the schedule that actually prove the show works.

This guide covers what to defend on the clock: priority rehearsal segments, change-control rules once cue-to-cue starts, and the vendor questions that keep rehearsal from turning into an unpaid tech check with executives waiting in the wing.

## Rehearsal day proves the show — not the gear

Load-in confirms inputs work. Rehearsal confirms cues land together — walk-on music into VOG, IMAG switch on the presenter mark, lighting preset on the reveal, mic handoff at the lectern. If those beats aren't in the run-of-show with sub-cues, rehearsal becomes a real-time rewrite while talent sits in holding.

Rehearsal is not the time to:

- **Finalize content** — slides, sizzle reels, and lower-thirds should be locked before doors to the ballroom; rehearsal tests playback, not creative direction.
- **Onboard new presenters** — anyone who hasn't seen the stage path, mark, and mic assignment burns time the show caller needs for turnover drills.
- **Re-debate scenic or rigging** — if a banner height blocks sight lines, that conversation belongs in pre-pro, not between cue-to-cue passes.
- **Run a client walk-through through the wing** — tours through stage left during a live segment reset are how cables get kicked and mic swaps get missed.

What rehearsal day must deliver: a run-of-show vFinal that every department has tested at show pace, with documented failover paths and a signed change window.

## Protect these blocks before anything else hits the schedule

Rehearsal hours compress fast — a delayed lunch, a CEO arrival pushed thirty minutes, and suddenly the panel turnover drill disappears. Rank your schedule in tiers and defend Tier 1 even when marketing wants "just one more photo op on the deck."

**Tier 1 — non-negotiable:**

- **Full cue-to-cue at show pace** — presenters at actual speed, applause and pauses included; a timecode-only run lies about program length.
- **Highest-risk segment isolation** — reveal, awards turnover, panel swap, or CEO keynote block run three times cold before the full run.
- **Failover drill** — kill primary playback, drop automation, or simulate a dead lav; confirm who calls backup and how long recovery takes.
- **IMAG and confidence monitor review** — watch switcher output on a monitor sized like your delay screens; what reads live can be a dark blob on the back row.
- **Run-of-show sign-off** — show caller, technical director, and client approver confirm vFinal; verbal "we're good" is not a sign-off.

**Tier 2 — defend if possible:**

- **Presenter turnover at pace** — two segments back-to-back with mic swap and wing traffic timed; if swap exceeds ninety seconds, the run-of-show is lying about your break.
- **Stream or overflow feed check** — separate preset from in-room IMAG; broadcast delay and graphics safe zones need their own pass.
- **Audio handoff sequence** — walk-on bed into VOG into lav into video audio; confirm ducking and that program mix matches what talent hears on IFB.

**Tier 3 — first cuts when time runs short:**

- **Scenic touch-up and brand photo blocks** — schedule after show proof, not inside cue-to-cue.
- **Redundant full runs** — a second complete pass only if the first surfaced document-level changes; otherwise run fixed segments.
- **Non-show departments' "quick tests"** — registration badge scanners and sponsor activations do not belong in the wing during turnover.

Put tiers in the rehearsal call sheet. When the client asks to add a thirty-minute "run the sizzle one more time for the board," you know exactly what gets displaced.

## Questions to bring your production vendor

A rehearsal brief that only lists "cue-to-cue at 2 PM" invites scope creep — and scope creep on rehearsal day always comes out of show proof. Bring these before the schedule is published:

- **Who owns the run-of-show during rehearsal** — show caller, production company, or shared; and who has final authority when client-side edits conflict with tested cues.
- **Rehearsal attendance matrix** — which segments require the actual CEO, awards presenter, or demo operator vs. a stand-in; mark "must attend" on the call sheet, not in a sidebar email.
- **Change window cutoff** — last time a run-of-show edit can be made without overtime, untested files, or a missed turnover drill; put the time on the document header.
- **Cue document version control** — one file name, one distribution list, one person who pushes updates; no parallel "client copy" with different cue numbers.
- **Comms structure for rehearsal** — show caller, wings, FOH, video shade, and A2 on defined channels; client walk-through traffic stays off the calling channel.
- **Overtime triggers** — what happens if Tier 1 blocks slip past the scheduled end; confirm crew rates and whether a second cue-to-cue window exists show morning.
- **Contingency for shortened rehearsal** — if arrival delays cut two hours, which Tier 1 blocks production runs anyway and which require a show-morning add.

Answers belong in the production brief and on the rehearsal call sheet distributed to security, talent wranglers, and client-side producers. Verbal agreements at the pre-pro meeting evaporate when three departments interpret "after the keynote" differently.

## Change control once cue-to-cue starts

Rehearsal day generates edits — a presenter adds thirty seconds, a video file gets renamed, a lighting look reads flat on IMAG. Uncontrolled edits are how a tested show becomes an untested show with an hour until doors.

Run-of-show change rules to enforce on site:

1. **All edits go through the show caller** — no wing-side cue tweaks, no "just tell lighting to hold the look" without a document update.
2. **Every change gets a version bump** — vFinal becomes vFinal.1 with a timestamp; department heads confirm they have the new file before the next pass.
3. **Segment-only re-runs after a change** — if the CEO block changed, re-run that block and the transition into the next segment; do not assume the full run still holds.
4. **No new content after the content cutoff** — if marketing delivers a slide at 4 PM, it plays from a preview monitor in holding, not from the wall, until tested on the actual canvas.
5. **Post-rehearsal freeze** — after sign-off, only safety or legal holds get edits; everything else waits for the post-mortem.

Planners who treat rehearsal as flexible creative time are the ones requesting a new lower-third package while the show caller is mid-turnover drill. Production can often accommodate it. They cannot accommodate discovering the font doesn't match the template at doors-open.

## From the floor: the hold that wasn't on the sheet

One corporate general session rehearsal ran the CEO block cleanly — walk-on, slides, Q&A, exit — and then held for "audience questions" that weren't in the run-of-show. Wing staff kept the next presenter at the mark. The show caller kept the house lights on a Q&A look. Video shade held a static slide. Two departments thought the hold was thirty seconds. Marketing thought it was open-ended.

The next segment started ninety seconds late. The panel turnover drill got cut. The mic swap that failed on show morning had been on the schedule until the hold ate it.

The fix was a single line in the run-of-show — "Q&A: max 3 questions, show caller calls hard out" — and a physical timer at the calling position. The CEO still took questions. The panel just got its drill back.

Rehearsal-day run-of-show is not about owning every fader move. It's about owning the clock — tiered blocks, change control, attendance rules, and the sign-offs that keep departments reading the same document when pressure starts.
