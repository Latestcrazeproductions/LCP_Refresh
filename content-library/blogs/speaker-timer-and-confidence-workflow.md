Timed keynotes fail in predictable ways: the speaker never sees the countdown, the stage manager calls "two minutes" into a dead mic, or the confidence feed shows slides but hides the timer behind presenter notes. A speaker timer workflow is not a single app on a laptop; it is who sees what, when warnings fire, and how that lines up with [confidence monitor placement](/blog/confidence-monitor-placement-for-keynotes) and slide advance.

This guide covers timer modes for corporate keynotes, how to surface countdowns on confidence without crowding the deck, and what to lock in the run-of-show before rehearsal so your general session stays on time without awkward cutoff music.

## What "speaker timer" means on a live show

**Program time** is the segment on the clock — doors, opener, CEO, panel. **Speaker time** is the slice inside that segment when one person owns the mic. **Stage time** often includes walk-on, applause, and exit; talent will quote slide count, not applause length. Your workflow has to separate those or every keynote runs three minutes long and the award segment eats the break.

Common timer surfaces:

- **Confidence monitor overlay** — countdown or bar burned into the presenter feed (downstage monitor or lectern)
- **Stage manager station** — large clock for the SM and show caller; may not be visible to the speaker
- **Follow operator display** — slide list plus elapsed time when someone other than the speaker advances
- **Backstage countdown** — wing monitor or tablet for intro talent waiting in the queue
- **Audio / lighting cues** — non-verbal "five minutes" (house dip, subtle bump) only when rehearsed with the speaker

Hardware timers (dedicated clock boxes, some switcher channels) and software timers (Presenter View, ProPresenter, QLab, dedicated show-control apps) both work. The failure mode is not the tool; it is two different clocks disagreeing while the speaker trusts the wrong one.

## Timer modes worth specifying in the brief

Do not leave "we need a timer" for load-in. Name the mode, the warning points, and what happens at zero.

**Countdown from allocated time** — Speaker gets 18:00; timer runs down. Best for fixed keynotes with a hard handoff to panel or video. Set warnings at 5:00, 2:00, and 0:30 unless talent asks otherwise; document whether zero means "finish the thought" or "hard stop."

**Count-up with soft cap** — Clock shows elapsed; SM tracks against run-of-show. Works for fireside chats and executives who resist visible countdowns. Trade-off: speaker has less urgency; SM must be willing to step in.

**Segment vs session timer** — General session may need a master clock (keynote + demos + recognition) plus a per-speaker reset. Panel formats need a timer per seat or a single moderator clock that pauses during audience questions.

**Hold and resume** — Applause, standing O, and unplanned remarks pause the countdown only if everyone agrees how. If only the SM pauses but the overlay keeps ticking, you will get a sidelong glance that is not gratitude.

**Pre-show and walk-on** — Decide whether the timer starts at walk-on, first word, or when slides appear. Walk-on variance is how 12-minute keynotes become 19-minute keynotes.

Questions to bring to your production partner:

- "Where does the speaker see warnings — confidence only, or also audio in-ear?"
- "Who owns pause: stage manager, follow, or director?"
- "At zero, do we dim house, cue music, or let show caller verbal?"
- "Do remote and in-room feeds share the same timing cues?"

## Confidence workflow — one feed, clear hierarchy

Confidence monitors already carry slides and sometimes notes. Adding a timer without a layout plan turns the downstage monitor into a spreadsheet. Treat timer visibility as a **workflow** tied to who advances and who calls time.

Lock this hierarchy before content lock:

1. **Primary confidence** — Slides (or presenter view) remain the largest element; timer sits in a consistent corner with high contrast, not over financial tables.
2. **Timer source of truth** — One system drives the on-screen clock; SM may run a secondary reference, but never a conflicting countdown on stage.
3. **Warning behavior** — Amber at five, red at two, flash at zero — or only color change with no flash. Rehearse with the speaker; surprise flashing reads as punishment on IMAG.
4. **Notes vs clock** — Presenter view with long notes may shrink the timer to illegible. Export a "show mode" layout or dedicate a second confidence for time only on long-form keynotes.
5. **Backup path** — If software timer dies, SM calls vocal cues on a comms loop the speaker can hear; wing tablet shows elapsed as backup.

Pair timer placement with [confidence monitor placement for keynotes](/blog/confidence-monitor-placement-for-keynotes): a timer on the wing monitor the speaker never uses is decoration. Rehearse on marks at show brightness.

Slide advance and timer must agree. If follow runs the deck from the booth while the speaker clicks a dead clicker, the timer and slides drift apart and the SM starts negotiating mid-sentence. Name advance authority in the run-of-show the same way you name wireless channels.

## Run-of-show locks for timed keynotes

Most overtime comes from missing paperwork, not from bad intentions. Put these in the production bible and the SM script:

**Allocated times** — Per segment, not per slide. Include walk-off and video roll; executives remember their speaking time, not your bumper.

**Warning script** — Exact language or cue type at 5:00, 2:00, 1:00, hold line. Verbal warnings in the lav are optional and must be rehearsed; unprepared "you have two minutes" in the room sounds like a reprimand on stream.

**Hard stop policy** — Music, LD cue, or SM walk-on? Legal and comms need to know if CEO remarks can be truncated for broadcast.

**Comms map** — SM to follow, SM to video, SM to speaker (if permitted). Timer disputes get resolved off-mic.

**Rehearsal beats** — Run at least one full timed pass with talent on confidence, warnings enabled, and house at show levels. Content-only rehearsal catches slides, not pacing.

**Executive briefing** — Five minutes explaining where the clock lives, what colors mean, and that pausing for applause is a choice, not an accident.

For multi-segment general sessions, tie speaker timers to [rehearsal day run-of-show](/blog/rehearsal-day-run-of-show-for-planners) practices: the first timed run is where you learn the opener always runs long.

## From the floor: the timer on the wrong output

A sales kickoff once fed a beautiful 20-minute countdown to the tech table — crisp font, correct warnings — while the CEO's confidence showed only mirror program, no overlay. The follow operator watched the clock; the speaker watched bullet three. At the two-minute warning nobody moved because the SM thought the overlay was live and follow thought vocal cues were enough.

Fix at rehearsal took four minutes: route presenter view with timer to the lectern monitor, program-only to the wing backup, and put the SM clock on a separate display. The keynote still ran 90 seconds over because applause was not in the allocation. The timer worked; the paperwork caught up the next year.

Speaker timers are simple. They fail when the countdown is visible to everyone except the person talking, when two operators run two clocks, or when warnings were never rehearsed with the person wearing the lav. Spec the mode, wire it into confidence on marks, and rehearse time like you rehearse audio.
