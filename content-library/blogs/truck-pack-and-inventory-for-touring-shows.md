A touring corporate show is only as consistent as the truck pack behind it. The same keynote look in four markets depends on one master inventory count, a defined load order, and a spare policy everyone follows — not whatever fit when the first venue called strike. When inventory lives in three spreadsheets and two vans, the missing RF receiver always shows up in the city with the earliest doors.

This guide covers how to build a touring truck pack and inventory list planners can audit: kit tiers per show format, what belongs on the master count sheet, load and strike sequence rules, and the production partner questions that catch shrinkage before leg two.

## One master list — not a quote and a prayer

Touring fails when each market quotes from a local mindset. Your production partner needs a single authoritative inventory: quantity, serial or asset tag, packed case ID, and which leg it ships on. Duplicates on paper do not mean duplicates on the truck — and "we'll grab another from the shop" does not scale when the shop is twelve hundred miles behind you.

Fields every touring master inventory should carry:

- **Asset class and line item** — Speaker model, truss stick count, RF channel bundle, cable kit type; enough specificity that a substitute requires a written swap, not a crew guess at load-in.
- **Quantity per kit tier** — Base show, breakout add-on, hybrid stream pack, executive lounge kit; tiers prevent leg-three scope creep from redefining the truck.
- **Case or flight-pack ID** — Numbered cases mapped to truck position and wing; loose gear in unlabeled bins is how LED processors arrive at the wrong dock.
- **Spare policy** — What travels on the truck as hot spare vs what stays at the shop as cold backup; spares consume weight and cube — they need a line item, not a corner of the truck.
- **Consumables and expendables** — Gaff, zip ties, batteries, lamp stock, RF tape; touring burns through these faster than single-venue shows and nobody restocks mid-route unless the list says so.
- **Market-specific exclusions** — Union labor rules, venue house gear, or weight limits that change what ships vs what gets procured locally; mark these on the list before the first pull sheet prints.

The master list is the contract between planner, production partner, and show caller. Change control runs through it — not through a side text on travel day.

## Truck pack order that survives strike chaos

Truck pack is logistics choreography: what loads last comes off first. Corporate touring usually mixes general-session core, breakouts, scenic, and cable infrastructure in one or two trailers. A pack built for "fit everything in" becomes a four-hour unload when the A1 needs the RF rack and it is behind the deck legs.

Standard load-order principles for touring corporate:

1. **First off / last on** — FOH and primary control racks, show-critical RF and playback, cable trunks for stage build, and the first-breakout kit for early room turnover. These ride at the nose or the door side of the trailer.
2. **Build sequence middle** — Deck legs and platform pieces, main truss or ground-support bundles, LED tile road cases in stack order, scenic that does not block case access.
3. **Last off / first on** — Cable ramps, dollies, consumable bins, strike tools, soft goods that absorb space in gaps. Power distro stays accessible through the load — nobody wants to pull twelve cases to reach a distro rack at 6 AM.
4. **Cross-dock and transfer legs** — When a leg flies instead of drives, mark which cases are air vs truck; air legs need weight and dimension on the list before booking freight.

Add a simple pack map: trailer bay or truck ID, case numbers per zone, and estimated unload sequence. Crew leads should read the map without asking the PM which door to open first.

## Kit tiers — hold the same show in every room

Corporate roadshows rarely run identical rooms; they run identical *shows*. Tiering keeps inventory honest when some markets add a partner lounge or drop a breakout.

**Tier definitions to lock before the first pull:**

- **Core plenary** — Stage deck count, PA main and delay, IMAG switcher path, primary LED or projection stack, lighting position package, show-caller comms, and executive IFB.
- **Breakout multiplier** — Standard breakout kit: projector or display, audio DSP for speech, HDMI matrix or switch, confidence monitor, tier-appropriate wireless pack, and cabling to a labeled length. Document how many concurrent breakouts the truck supports without a second trailer.
- **Hybrid / capture add-on** — Encoders, ISO record paths, return-feed hardware, backup internet path; list separately so a non-stream leg does not pay cube for gear that stays in the case.
- **Evening or awards extension** — Band backline, supplemental lighting, scenic deltas; if not every leg uses it, say which cases stay "cold" and who pays remanifest fees when they open.

When marketing adds a breakout on leg four, the question is not "can we find gear" — it is whether tier inventory has another breakout kit *on the truck* or whether you are building from venue house stock and praying the connectors match.

## Questions for your touring production partner

Bring this list to pre-pro and again at mid-tour review. Vague answers mean the inventory list is still aspirational.

- **Single source of truth** — Who owns the live master list, where is it hosted, and how are mid-tour swaps logged with client visibility?
- **Asset tagging and checkout** — Are cases scanned at load and strike, and who signs off when a spare leaves the truck and does not return?
- **Freight and insurance** — Per-leg coverage, declared values matched to the list, and process when a case is short-received at the dock.
- **Local supplement policy** — What is allowed to source in market (cable, truss, displays) without breaking look-and-feel, and how are those items merged into the master count for the next leg?
- **Weight and cube limits** — Maximum trailer payload per leg, overhead rigging reduction plans when weight caps bite, and who re-packs when a venue bans drive-in loads.
- **Strike inventory check** — Is there a mandatory count before wheels roll, and what is the escalation path when counts do not match?

Answers belong in the touring production agreement and on the pack map — not in a recap email after the third missing handheld.

## From the floor: the case that was "probably on the other truck"

A multi-market partner summit ran two identical breakout tiers from one trailer through six legs. Strike in the third market was clean until the overnight count: one Tier 2 wireless kit was short a receiver, and the spare labeled "BREAKOUT 2" was actually empty — someone had pulled it for a general-session backup in leg one and never relabeled the case.

The production manager found a local rental by 7 AM. The show sounded fine. The planner's discovery was quieter: the master list still showed twelve receivers; the truck had eleven and a foam insert with no asset tag. They spent the next leg building a strike ritual — photo of each open case, signer on the dock, and no wheels before counts match. The PM called it "truck yoga." It worked.

Touring corporate production is inventory discipline in motion. Build one master list, tier your kits, pack for unload order, and treat strike count like a cue you do not skip.
