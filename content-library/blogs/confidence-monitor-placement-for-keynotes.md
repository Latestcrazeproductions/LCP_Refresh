Confidence monitors exist so your speaker reads slides without turning their back to the audience or squinting at a 40-foot LED. Placement is a workflow decision: where the presenter stands, where cameras shoot, and how bright the room runs during the general session.

This guide covers what confidence monitors do versus IMAG and the hero wall, where to put them for common keynote formats, and what to lock before rehearsal so your CEO is not negotiating sight lines ten minutes before doors.

## Jobs of confidence vs IMAG vs the hero wall

Three different surfaces, three different audiences. Mixing them up is how you get a presenter reading bullet points off the IMAG feed while the audience watches the back of their head.

The **hero wall** (LED or projection) is for the room and the stream. It carries slides, video rolls, and — when you have a camera package — switched IMAG shots. It is not a private teleprompter unless you enjoy watching executives pivot 90 degrees mid-sentence.

**IMAG** is image magnification for the audience: switched camera shots of faces, panels, and demos on the main display. It requires cameras, a shader, and rehearsal. Confidence monitors do not replace IMAG; they replace the bad habit of using the hero wall as a personal notes screen.

**Confidence monitors** (downstage monitors, lectern monitors, or a dedicated presenter feed) show the speaker their slides, timer, and sometimes the next cue — without requiring them to look at the audience-facing wall. The feed is usually a duplicate program output or a separate "presenter view" with notes and upcoming builds.

If your only problem is back-row legibility, you may need [delay screens or IMAG](/blog/when-you-need-imag-and-delay-screens), not another confidence monitor. If your problem is the speaker missing animation cues because they cannot see their own deck, confidence placement is the fix.

## Placement options — where to put the feed

There is no universal "best" position. The right placement follows stage format, camera blocking, and whether the speaker roams or stays at a lectern. Use these options as a starting spec in your production brief:

- **Downstage floor monitors (left and/or right)** — Classic keynote setup: one or two low-profile displays at the downstage edge, angled up toward the presenter mark. Works for roaming speakers who pass through center stage; pair with a marked "home" position so they know where to glance. Keep them low enough that audience sight lines stay clean and cameras on a waist shot do not frame a glowing rectangle.
- **Lectern-integrated monitor** — Built into or clamped on the lectern, slightly below eye line. Best for fixed remarks, scripted openers, and executives who will not leave the podium. Confirm the lectern position in rehearsal — a monitor aimed at a mark six feet away from where the speaker actually stands is a confidence monitor in name only.
- **Offstage side monitor** — A display on the wing, readable with a slight head turn. Useful when downstage floor monitors would spill into the first camera or when a deep thrust puts the presenter too close to the lip for floor units. Trade-off: every glance reads as "looking offstage" on IMAG unless the camera package knows to avoid that angle during key beats.
- **Upstage confidence (avoid unless you must)** — Monitor behind the speaker, between them and the hero wall. Tempting when downstage space is tight; usually a mistake because the speaker turns away from the audience to read. If production insists, limit to backup and keep the primary feed downstage or in the lectern.
- **Presenter-view app or backup tablet** — iPad or laptop on a stand with slide notes, timer, and next-slide preview. Not a substitute for a properly placed monitor on a bright stage, but a solid backup when the primary feed fails or when a fireside format makes floor monitors awkward. Lock brightness, Do Not Disturb, and charging before show call.

For panel formats, the moderator often needs a separate confidence feed from panelists — shared slide view plus a timer and upcoming segment. Do not assume one downstage monitor serves five people at a table.

## Brightness, eye line, and camera spill

A confidence monitor that is too dim washes out under stage lighting; one that is too bright becomes the second sun and pulls the speaker's gaze like a bug zapper. Eye line matters as much as brightness — presenters should read with a slight downward glance, not a visible wobble toward the floor or a full turn to the wing.

Lock these before focus:

- **Brightness target** — Match the monitor to ambient stage light at show levels, not at focus with house at 100%. Video shade or the LED processor often controls the presenter feed; confirm who adjusts it when the LD drops house for walk-in.
- **Eye-line angle** — Ideal read is 10–20° below horizontal from the primary mark; steeper angles look like the speaker is staring at their shoes on camera.
- **Spill and reflection** — Glossy lectern surfaces, teleprompter glass, and glasses on the speaker can bounce monitor content into the hero camera. Flag it in rehearsal, not in the opening cue.
- **Content on the feed** — Presenter view with notes and next slide, or clean duplicate program? Executives who have never seen "presenter mode" need five minutes in rehearsal, not a tutorial during the house countdown.
- **Camera awareness** — Tell the video director which monitor is primary so wide shots are not scheduled while the speaker is mid-glance at the wing monitor.

Confidence monitors also interact with [stage deck sizing](/blog/stage-deck-sizing-for-panels-and-keynotes): a deck that is too shallow pushes floor monitors into the first row's sight line; a deck that is too narrow leaves no wing for a side monitor when cameras already own both sides.

## What to lock in the run-of-show

Most squinting and off-wall reading happens because confidence placement was treated as an AV afterthought. Name it in the run-of-show and the production brief the same way you name wireless mics and slide aspect ratio.

Specify before load-in:

1. **Primary and backup feed** — Which monitor is show-critical, and what happens if it dies (second monitor, tablet, stage manager calling slide numbers).
2. **Lectern and mark positions** — Physical tape on deck, not "about here" after VIP walk-through.
3. **Who advances slides** — Clicker, stage manager, or follow operator; confidence is useless if the feed and the clicker disagree.
4. **Timer visibility** — Countdown on confidence, on a stage manager station, or both; when the "two minutes" cue fires, where does the speaker see it.
5. **Rehearsal block with talent on marks** — Minimum ten minutes with the speaker at the actual lectern and walk path, house at show brightness, cameras on.
6. **IMAG shot plan during reads** — Wide vs tight when the speaker references a slide; avoid cutting to a profile while they are clearly reading from the wing monitor.

Questions worth asking your staging vendor in the bid review:

- "For a keynote like ours, where do you typically place confidence relative to the hero wall processor location?"
- "Who shades or dims the presenter feed when we drop house?"
- "What is your default when the speaker asks to move the lectern during rehearsal?"

## From the floor: the monitor that faced the camera

One general session shipped two perfect downstage confidence monitors and a CEO who preferred to stand upstage left, half turned toward the hero wall, reading animation cues off the LED because it "felt more natural." The monitors worked. The mark did not.

Rehearsal had been content-only — slides advanced, audio checked, no one on stage. Show caller caught it at five minutes to doors. The quick fix was rotating one floor monitor and accepting that the opening wide shot would include a brief glance downstage. The real fix would have been holding the lectern and enforcing a mark the day before.

Confidence monitors are not complicated technology. They fail when placement follows a scenic render instead of a speaker, when brightness is set at focus instead of show look, and when the run-of-show never assigns them an owner. Spec the placement, rehearse with the person who will stand there, and keep the hero wall for the audience.
