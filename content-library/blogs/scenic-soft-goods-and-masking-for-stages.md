Soft goods and masking turn a ballroom rig into a stage the audience reads as intentional. They hide cable troughs, empty wings, and the back of your LED wall. They frame sight lines, control spill from lighting and projection, and give brand color somewhere to live when the screen content goes dark.

This guide covers what to specify for legs, borders, teasers, and scrims on corporate general sessions, how much masking you actually need for cameras and house sight lines, and the questions that keep drape from becoming a last-minute truck add.

## What soft goods do on a corporate stage

On corporate shows, soft goods are rarely decorative for its own sake. They solve visibility, camera, and safety problems that hard scenic cannot fix quickly at load-in.

Plan masking around four jobs:

- **Hide the mechanics** — truss legs, ground-support LED frames, cable ramps, distro, and the gap between deck edge and ballroom carpet.
- **Control light and spill** — black drape eats stray key light and keeps projection surfaces from picking up wash; white or gray legs bounce fill when you want a brighter upstage without adding fixtures.
- **Define the proscenium** — teasers and borders shorten the ceiling visually and give IMAG a clean top line; legs set the width the audience believes is "the stage."
- **Carry brand without fighting content** — logo legs, color-matched velour, or a scrim layer for a walk-in look that is not a PowerPoint slide.

If your brief only says "black drape," vendors will default to whatever width their pipe and drape kit supports — not what your side cameras or delay screens need.

## Masking checklist — width, height, and depth

Masking fails when it is sized for the deck but not for the full production footprint. Measure and specify in three dimensions: how wide the opening reads, how tall the trim line sits, and how deep masking runs upstage to hide wings and tech.

Run this before you approve a soft-goods package:

- **Leg positions and count** — pair of legs at stage width minimum; add break legs or chevron masking when scenic or LED eats wing clearance and the audience sees sidelight stands.
- **Teaser height** — bottom of teaser relative to lighting trim and projector throw; a teaser hung too low clips followspot paths; too high and the ballroom ceiling dominates on camera.
- **Border or full black** — borders for a defined "picture frame"; full black upstage when the LED wall is the only scenic and you need to kill reflection off ballroom walls.
- **Upstage drape or sharkstooth scrim** — full black upstage to hide crossovers and presenter entrances; scrim when you want a reveal or backlight silhouette look in walk-in.
- **Deck skirt and stage edge** — velour skirt on deck legs, black masking on step units, and a plan for where cable trough meets carpet (gaffer is not a long-term skirt strategy).
- **Camera sight lines** — side cameras shooting across stage should not see the backstage gap between leg and wall; walk the positions during pre-pro, not after drape is tied.
- **Fire and venue rules** — flame-retardant certificates, black-out vs dim-out requirements, and whether the venue bans certain hang methods or requires their labor for anything overhead.

Ceiling height and rigging points cap teaser and border options before fabric choice does. Send the ballroom section drawing with leg locations marked — not just a stage render.

## Package tiers — when to scale soft goods up or down

Soft-goods packages should track show format, camera count, and how much of the room is visible from the house. Use these tiers in budget conversations; adjust for union labor and venue hang restrictions.

**Lean (single keynote, one LED or projection surface, no side cameras in wings):**

- Pair of black legs at proscenium width, teaser if trim allows, deck skirt on exposed deck faces
- Upstage black drape or existing ballroom black masking reused with production legs in front
- Skip scrim unless brand wants a walk-in look separate from screen content

**Standard (panel, IMAG, two or more cameras, ground-support scenic):**

- Legs plus borders, full upstage black, wing masking deep enough to hide crossovers and monitor world
- Break legs or additional masking at LED or scenic returns so side shots do not show truck cases
- Coordinated color: black velour at spill zones, brand or gray legs only where lighting can control wash

**Heavy (awards, multi-segment general session, broadcast or stream with tight shots):**

- Full box masking around performance area, teasers/borders sized for camera safe title, dedicated masking for B-camera cross-shoot lanes
- Scrims or layered goods for segment transitions; spare drape for cable paths that move between keynote and panel reset
- Pre-rigged fold or traveler if quick reveals are in the run of show — confirm motor or hand operation in rehearsal

Scaling down is valid when the room already provides effective black and your show is room-only with no IMAG. Scaling up is mandatory when a single wide shot can see backstage — streams do not forgive gaps the live audience ignores.

## Spec lines and questions for your scenic vendor

Most soft-goods RFPs list "pipe and drape" with a dollar line. Few list leg spacing, fabric type, or who hangs and strikes relative to LED and audio.

Add these to your production brief:

- **Fabric** — black velour vs Encore or similar dim-out; denier and nap direction if projection or side light grazes the surface
- **Dimensions** — leg height and width, teaser drop, upstage run depth, and distance from deck edge to downstage leg
- **Hardware** — pipe size, base type (ballroom floor rules), sandbag count, and whether anything ties into deck or ground-support
- **Integration** — who masks around LED frame, who skirts deck after furniture lands, and order of operations with [stage deck sizing](/blog/stage-deck-sizing-for-panels-and-keynotes) and cable trough
- **Certifications** — flame certificate copies for venue submission and label photos if the fire marshal asks on show day

Questions worth asking in bid review:

1. **"Walk me through wing masking for a panel with side cameras — how deep do your legs need to sit upstage of the deck?"**
2. **"What do you do when the LED wall is wider than our proscenium legs — break legs, extended teaser, or hard scenic?"**
3. **"Who owns the gap between deck skirt and carpet once cable ramps move for a reset?"**
4. **"What is your default when the venue will not allow your hang — floor-supported masking only, or venue drape plus your legs?"**

A vendor who answers with room depth and camera positions — not only linear feet of drape — has been on a corporate general session floor.

## From the floor: the leg that was exactly ten feet

One general session shipped legs sized to the deck width on the order — twenty-four feet between pipes — while the LED wall and ground-support frame spanned thirty-two. From the house, the extra eight feet of hardware read as a gray stripe on every wide shot. The side camera caught case stacks the legs were supposed to hide because upstage black stopped at the deck edge, not at the crossover path.

Fixing it meant a second truck run for break legs and an hour of re-hang while audio finished line check — not because anyone forgot drape, but because masking was spec'd to the render's stage box, not to the production footprint. The teaser was fine. The teaser was not the problem.

Soft goods are cheap relative to LED and labor until they are wrong. Spec masking to cameras, wings, and resets — not just to the deck top view. The audience may not name velour. They notice when the stage looks like a ballroom with a screen dropped in front.
