Hotel ballrooms run on banquet schedules, not show schedules. When your production vendor and the hotel's catering team both need the same room at 2 PM, the contract decides who wins — and whether your LED wall gets built before the first coffee urn rolls in. A load-in timeline that lives only in a Slack thread is not a plan; it is a bet that the freight elevator will be empty when you need it.

This guide covers what to put in the venue contract and production rider before anyone signs: dock and elevator ownership, hour-by-hour sequencing for a typical general session build, contract vs rider split, and strike order so teardown does not fight the morning plenary reset.

## Dock, freight elevator, and who owns the room when

Hotel ballrooms share infrastructure with every other event on property that week. Production needs continuous access; banquet needs flip time; engineering needs the loading dock for linen trucks. Without named windows in the contract, "load-in starts at 8 AM" means eight different things to eight different departments.

Lock these ownership decisions in writing before you hold dates:

- **Loading dock assignment** — Which bay(s) your trucks use, maximum vehicle length, and whether the dock is shared with other groups on your load-in day. Include a backup bay if the primary is blocked.
- **Freight elevator windows** — Start and end times, weight capacity, pad requirements, and whether house staff or your crew operates the lift. Banquet often books the same elevator for chair stacks at 3 PM.
- **Ballroom access tiers** — When production may enter with cases, when rigging may begin, when soft goods (drapes, carpet) may go down, and when banquet may reset tables. These are separate permissions, not one "room available" line.
- **House vs production labor boundaries** — Who moves furniture, who resets room dividers, who handles power tie-ins, and whether union rules apply to your vendor's crew on property.
- **Single point of contact** — One named hotel operations manager with authority to resolve conflicts on show day, reachable on a direct line — not the sales coordinator who sold the block.

## Hour-by-hour sample timeline for a general session build

Timelines vary by show scale, but the sequence repeats: power and rigging before scenic, scenic before tech, tech before content. Banquet inserts flip windows wherever the contract allows — and production must build backward from doors-open, not forward from "when the truck arrives."

Sample timeline for a 500-person general session with staging, LED wall, and full AV — load-in day before a 7:30 AM rehearsal:

1. **6:00–8:00 AM — Soft access, no rigging** — Cases to staging storage, FOH position marked, power distro layout confirmed with engineering. No motors, no lifts, no furniture moves without banquet sign-off.
2. **8:00–10:00 AM — Rigging and power** — Motors hung, truss in place, primary power drops verified. Banquet holds the floor clear; no chair delivery in the ballroom during this window.
3. **10:00 AM–12:00 PM — Stage and scenic** — Deck assembled, LED wall or projection surface erected, drape and branding installed. Freight elevator reserved for wall panels or large scenic — not concurrent with linen delivery.
4. **12:00–2:00 PM — AV build** — Audio, video, lighting hung and cabled. FOH and monitor world established. Banquet may prep adjacent pre-function space but not the ballroom floor.
5. **2:00–4:00 PM — Focus, programming, RF** — Lighting focus, audio tuning, wireless scan and coordination. Content playback paths tested. This is not the window for banquet to reset a lunch setup in the same room.
6. **4:00–6:00 PM — Technical rehearsal block** — Full cue-to-cue with switching, IMAG, and comms live. Any banquet access during this block requires production approval and a documented pause.
7. **6:00 PM onward — Hold or limited access** — Either production locks the room for overnight, or a written limited-access window allows only preset adjustments. No "quick chair count" without a show-caller sign-off.

Adjust the clock for your show, but keep the order. Rigging after scenic is how a 16-foot wall becomes a 14-foot wall with a ceiling pocket nobody measured.

## Venue contract vs production rider — who documents what

Planners often assume the production vendor's rider covers hotel logistics. It does not — or it should not, because the hotel will not sign your vendor's document. Split responsibilities so the venue contract holds access and the production rider holds technical execution.

**Belongs in the venue contract (hotel signs):**

- Load-in and strike dates with start/end times for dock, freight elevator, and ballroom
- Exclusive or shared access language for each space (ballroom, pre-function, green room, dock)
- Penalty or remedy language when hotel operations blocks contracted access
- Power availability, tie-in location, and engineering contact for show day
- Room flip responsibilities — who moves tables, who stores chairs, who resets air walls
- Insurance and certificate of insurance requirements for production vendors on property
- Noise curfew and after-hours access fees, if applicable

**Belongs in the production rider (vendor signs):**

- Equipment list, truck count, and estimated load-in crew size (feeds hotel dock planning)
- Rigging plot, weight loads, and motor placement requiring engineering approval
- Power draw by circuit and distro layout
- Strike timeline and case storage location until pickup
- Crew call times aligned to — not contradicting — the venue contract windows
- Change-order triggers when hotel access slips and overtime crew is required

When the two documents disagree, the cheaper document loses — and that is usually yours. Send the hotel contract access windows to your production partner before they quote crew days. A vendor quoting a six-hour build when the contract allows four is pricing reality; a vendor quoting four when the hotel only guarantees two is pricing hope.

## Strike order before the first case comes off the truck

Strike is load-in in reverse, under tighter clocks, often while banquet is already mentally setting tomorrow's breakfast. Document strike sequence in the production rider and confirm it with hotel operations during advance — not at midnight when the housekeeper needs the floor.

Strike sequence checklist to include in advance materials:

- **Content and show systems first** — Playback shut down, switching powered off, wireless collected and counted, show files backed up if applicable.
- **Lighting and audio hang** — Fixtures struck in reverse order of hang; cable bundled by run, not coiled on the deck for the next show to trip over.
- **Video and LED** — Wall powered down and disassembled per manufacturer sequence; processors and sending cards packed before structural cases move.
- **Scenic and staging** — Branding, drape, and deck struck; marks removed; floor protection pulled before furniture returns.
- **Rigging last** — Motors and truss come down only after everything beneath is clear; hotel engineering sign-off if required.
- **Case movement windows** — When cases may cross the ballroom floor, when the freight elevator is reserved for outbound, and where empty cases stage until the truck returns.

Banquet gets the room back only when production and hotel ops both sign a floor release — not when the last tech walks out and assumes someone else will notice.

## From the floor: the general session that shared an elevator with a wedding

A corporate general session booked a single freight elevator from 10 AM to 4 PM — clear on the production rider, never written into the hotel contract. At 1 PM, catering opened the same lift for a wedding reception load on another floor: chair carts, then linen, then a cake table that did not fit beside a 40-panel LED road case.

Production finished the wall build by 5:30. Rehearsal started at 6. The planner had a signed BEO and a vendor timeline; the hotel had a wedding sales sheet and one elevator. Nobody lost the show — they lost the buffer that makes shows feel calm.

The fix was boring and effective: elevator windows in the venue contract, a named ops contact, and a strike sequence that assumed the wedding would still be loading at 11 PM. Hotels do not malice-block load-in. They optimize for the next event in the book unless your contract tells them which event that is.
