Outdoor AV fails when weather triggers are vague. “We’ll monitor the forecast” is not a plan; neither is a production rider that assumes dry grass and zero wind. This guide lists what to put in writing before load-in — venue agreement, vendor contract, and run-of-show addendum — so someone with authority can call a hold, a shelter move, or a show pause without renegotiating scope at the loading dock.

## Why weather belongs in the contract, not a hallway conversation

Weather decisions need a named owner, a clock, and thresholds everyone signed before the deposit was non-refundable. Banquet and venue sales teams often sell “rain or shine” while production is pricing wind-rated ballast, tent flaps that block PA dispersion, and labor for a midnight strike when lightning enters the forecast.

Split the documents the same way you would for power and rigging. The **venue or site agreement** covers shelter access, tent tie-in rights, indoor fallback space hold times, and fees if you move under cover. The **production contract** covers gear protection, crew safety stops, overtime tied to weather holds, and who pays for reconfiguration when wind limits drop your flown PA to ground stack. The **run-of-show addendum** (planner-owned, signed by show management and production) lists decision times, communication paths, and what changes on the timeline when you flip to Plan B.

When all three reference the same numbers — wind speed, lightning radius, precipitation start — you have something enforceable when marketing wants the show live and the rigging lead will not put a human on a lift.

## Weather thresholds to define before anyone signs

Vague language creates two bad outcomes: production shuts down while leadership argues about brand exposure, or crew keeps working in conditions your insurance broker would not endorse. Write measurable triggers and tie each to an action, not a mood.

**Minimum thresholds to document:**

1. **Wind** — Maximum sustained and gust limits for flown speakers, LED walls, lighting truss, and tent sidewalls. Include the anemometer source (on-site meter vs. airport feed) and who reads it.
2. **Lightning** — Radius and dwell time that trigger an all-clear hold (common production practice: 30-minute clock after last strike within a defined mile radius). Name who resets the clock.
3. **Precipitation** — Start/stop rules for powered gear on open deck, cable ramps, and FOH under tent. Define “misting” vs. “show stop” if your market cares about the distinction.
4. **Temperature and humidity** — Extremes that affect gear (condensation on lenses, projector/laser derate) and crew (heat index breaks). Outdoor summer keynotes fail on fogged IMAG before they fail on rain.
5. **Decision clock** — Fixed times when show management and production meet on the forecast (e.g., T-24, T-6, doors minus two hours) with pre-agreed outcomes: full show, modified show, shelter move, or hard cancel.

Attach a one-page **weather matrix** to the contract: threshold in the left column, mandatory action in the right, authority to call it in the footer. If the matrix lives only in a Slack thread, it is not a plan.

## Gear, shelter, and power — contract asks production needs

Outdoor shows are inventory and topology problems. Rain covers and sandbags are not “nice to have”; they are line items tied to liability. Shelter fallback is not “the hotel lobby if we need it” unless someone put that room on hold in writing.

**Put these in the production scope or technical addendum:**

- **Rated deployment** — Ground ballast weights or guy points for PA, LED, and FOH tent; wind derate for sidewall removal; policy on dropping scrim or soft goods that sail.
- **Environmental protection** — Rain hats for speakers and consoles, tent drip paths away from power distro, covered cases for open rack gear, desiccant and wipe-down protocol before power-on after a cell passes.
- **Cable and egress** — Covered cable ramps, GFCI policy, no coiled energised cable in standing water, defined path if the audience moves under cover mid-show.
- **Shelter Plan B** — Indoor or tented alternate with power one-line, ceiling height, noise curfew, and load-in window if you pivot from lawn to ballroom. Include who pays for re-hang vs. ground-stack redeploy.
- **Stream and record path** — If weather forces a shortened show or shelter move, confirm encoder and capture stay hot through the transition or define a hard pause with audience messaging owned by show management.

Ask for photos of the fallback room during advance, not a floor plan alone. A shelter that cannot fit your LED depth is a morale event, not a contingency.

## Questions to bring to venue, production, and show management

Use these on the kickoff call and require written answers in the contract pack or signed addendum — not verbal assurances from a site tour taken on a sunny Tuesday.

1. **Who has authority to pause or cancel for weather, and who can override them?** Name roles, not departments.
2. **What wind and lightning numbers trigger a mandatory crew hold?** If the answer is “OSHA,” push for site-specific limits tied to your plot.
3. **Where does gear go if a cell hits during load-in vs. during show?** Staging, covered dock, and truck pack-out sequences should be pre-approved.
4. **Is indoor fallback space contracted, keyed, and available for the full rehearsal window — or only after banquet teardown?**
5. **How are weather holds billed — straight time, minimum call, reconfiguration lump sum?** Cap exposure in the production agreement.
6. **What is the guest and talent communication script when you delay doors or cut a segment?** AV cannot improvise messaging while resetting FOH under a flap.
7. **What advance deliverables prove the outdoor plot was reviewed against forecast seasonality?** Prevailing wind rose, sun angle at call time, and tent HVAC noise on lav mics belong in the packet.

Venues and vendors that answer with documents get short-listed. Vendors that answer with “we’ve done outdoor before” get a matrix in the next revision.

## From the floor: when “rain plan” meant a tarp and optimism

A corporate lawn show booked a headline act, a ground-supported LED wall, and a FOH tent with open sides — because the render looked better without walls. The contract said “weather contingency per venue policy.” Venue policy was a laminated card about seeking shelter in the parking structure.

At T-2 hours the gust forecast crossed the plot limit for the wall. Production wanted sidewalls and ballast checks. Marketing wanted doors on time. Nobody had signing authority on the weather matrix because there was not one. The compromise was a delayed doors call, a half-wall FOH that still leaked on the console, and a line array that got rain-hatted mid-set while someone held an umbrella over the monitor desk. The show finished. The archive audio had wind rumble on every ballad.

The following year the same program shipped with a signed matrix, a shelter hold in the hotel ballroom, wind limits in the rigging plot, and a single show caller who could pause without a committee. Same lawn. Different paperwork — and a load-in where the only debate was whether the anemometer or the airport feed was lying, which is at least a measurable argument.

Outdoor AV is not luck. It is thresholds, shelter, and authority written down before the first case hits grass.
