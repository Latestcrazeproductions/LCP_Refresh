A show caller is not a nicer title for the person who already runs your switcher. They own sequence, timing, and intercom discipline while department heads execute lighting, audio, video, and stage traffic against a numbered cue sheet. This guide maps when that role belongs in the budget, when your technical director or stage manager can absorb it without blowing the run-of-show, and what to document so finance sees accountability — not another day rate on the quote.

## Business outcomes a show caller protects

Finance rarely approves a dedicated caller because someone wants a second headset. They approve it when a visible miss costs more than the labor line — schedule slip on the general session, executive time on a broken walk-on, or marketing assets that cannot be salvaged in post because the program mix was improvised.

The outcomes worth naming in the brief:

- **Schedule integrity** — Flagship segments start and end inside the window leadership committed to. Holds, skips, and F&B handoffs are logged with rationale, not negotiated from the calling position mid-cue.
- **Single sequence authority** — One person reads the run-of-show, calls standbys and executes, and owns recovery when the CEO runs long or the wrong deck hits the screen. Department heads do not freelance parallel timelines.
- **Comms hygiene** — Show caller, TD, LD, A1, V1, and stage management share a channel plan. Client walk-through traffic and wing chatter stay off the cue loop.
- **Capture-ready program flow** — Camera cuts, IMAG holds, and stream handoffs land on documented sub-cues so the director is not guessing while the room watches a hunting slide.

When those outcomes are contract requirements — not aspirations — the show caller line item is part of delivering the show, not production luxury.

## Complexity tiers: when to budget a dedicated show caller

Not every general session needs a caller who does nothing else. The trigger is simultaneous jobs under time pressure with an audience that notices seams.

Budget a dedicated show caller when two or more of these show up on the program:

- **Multi-department sync in the same beat** — Walk-on music, follow-spot, IMAG preset, lower-third, and mic state change in under ten seconds; more than twice in the same hour.
- **Awards, auction, or paddle-raise blocks** — Presenter turnover, envelope or bid mechanics, and lighting/IMAG reset between categories; turnover target under ninety seconds.
- **Hybrid or multi-destination output** — In-room IMAG, overflow, and stream or broadcast feed that do not share identical switcher presets; handoffs must be called, not discovered in rehearsal.
- **Separate F&B and show clocks** — Plated service, dessert clearance, or bar close tied to program milestones; someone must ping banquet without breaking cue discipline.
- **Executive or celebrity segments with hard holds** — Teleprompter, confidence monitor, and security or handler traffic; the caller shields the TD from becoming the emcee by accident.
- **Compressed load-in with no dark rehearsal day** — Cue-to-cue runs the same day as doors; there is no slack for the TD to call and shade simultaneously.

Tier down — shared role or stage-manager-led calling — when the show is one room, one projection or LED surface, two mics, and a linear slide deck with no live turnover. That format still needs a run-of-show owner. It does not always need a dedicated caller on the org chart.

## When a TD or stage manager can carry calling

Doubling the TD as show caller is a common budget compromise. It works until it does not — usually the first time a live switch, a playback fail, and a "can we add a video?" request arrive in the same thirty seconds.

Keep calling inside the TD or stage manager role when:

- **The TD is not on the switcher for the whole show** — They can sit at the calling position with comms to a dedicated shader; switching is not their primary hands-on job during cues.
- **The stage manager already owns a numbered cue sheet** — Corporate formats with fixed presenter order, minimal playback, and no auction or awards turnover; SM discipline matches show-caller discipline.
- **Rehearsal proved single-thread execution** — Full cue-to-cue at show pace with the same person calling and no uncalled holds longer than fifteen seconds.
- **Program changes are frozen** — Change window closed; no client-side "small" adds that require re-sequencing lighting and graphics on show day.

Red flags that shared calling will cost more than the day rate:

- TD calling from the switcher during the product reveal or CEO Q&A
- No backup caller if the primary is on a ladder fixing a playback path
- Stage manager wrangling presenters while also executing numbered cues to LD and V1
- Run-of-show edits after final cue-to-cue without a dedicated re-run block

If any of those appear in the pre-pro notes, split the roles in the budget before the PO is signed — not on load-in morning.

## Scope the line item without double-paying

Vendors sometimes bundle "show management" across TD, stage manager, and show caller without defining who sits where. Planners get three names and one person at the calling position. Align scope to the quote.

Bring this checklist to budget review and vendor negotiation:

1. **Name the calling position** — Who is at the show-caller station for cue-to-cue and show? If the answer is "whoever is free," the line item is not scoped.
2. **Define comms and cue document ownership** — Final run-of-show version, numbered cues, and who distributes updates after rehearsal.
3. **Separate F&B milestones from technical cues** — Show caller pings banquet; banquet does not negotiate dessert clearance on the show channel.
4. **Clarify TD vs caller split** — TD owns switcher, playback, and failover paths; caller owns sequence and talent timing. Document overlap only where rehearsed.
5. **Travel vs local** — Touring programs should name whether the caller travels the route or is hired per market; cue drift between cities is a real cost.
6. **Rehearsal hours tied to the role** — Flagship segments need cue-to-cue time with the actual caller, not a substitute during load-in.

## What leadership should see before approval

Marketing and events can save the CFO a circular debate by attaching one page that ties the line item to measurable risk:

- **Flagship segment named** — The general session, launch moment, or awards block that cannot slip on camera.
- **Complexity tier** — Which triggers from the list above apply; honest count, not wishful thinking.
- **Accountability chart** — TD, show caller, stage manager, client point; one show-day decision maker for sequence.
- **Rehearsal minimum** — Cue-to-cue hours with the calling position filled by the same person who works show.
- **Contingency** — Backup caller or SM deputy when the primary is tied up on a failover.

That brief does not guarantee applause. It guarantees nobody asks "why did we pay for two headsets?" after a clean CEO walk-on.

## From the floor: hold on channel two

A partner summit ran hybrid with a shared TD-caller on paper. Rehearsal was tight until marketing added a remote CEO join and a live demo with a floor handoff — same hour as the partner awards block. Show day, the TD was on the switcher for the demo while channel two asked for a hold on the walk-on music. The stage manager answered. Three departments interpreted "hold" differently for eleven seconds.

Nothing failed on stream. The house saw it. After lunch, finance approved a dedicated caller for the next two cities on the tour — same show file, less improvisation.

The line item was cheaper than explaining why the partner segment started on a still slide.
