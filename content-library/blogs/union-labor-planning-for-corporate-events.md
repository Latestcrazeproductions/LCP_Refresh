Union labor rules show up in venue contracts long before your production vendor loads in — and the questions you skip at site selection become change orders, compressed rehearsal, or a dock steward who starts the clock while two trucks argue over the same bay. This guide covers what corporate event planners should ask early about union jurisdiction, crew minimums, load-in windows, and who can actually touch your gear — not a labor-law textbook, but the practical checklist that keeps your general session on schedule.

Use it during venue shortlisting, contract review, and production RFP writing so labor assumptions are priced and scheduled before you sign.

## When union rules apply — and who actually enforces them

Union labor at corporate events typically flows through the venue, not your production company. Hotels, convention centers, arenas, and some municipal facilities operate under collective bargaining agreements that define which trades handle rigging, power, carpentry, and stagehand work — and which tasks your vendor crew can perform without a steward in the room.

Three layers matter before you book:

- **Venue jurisdiction** — Which union locals hold the contract (IATSE stagehands, IBEW electricians, riggers, carpenters). The venue's labor agreement, not your vendor's home market, sets the rules.
- **Exclusive vendor clauses** — Some properties require house AV, house rigging, or in-house electricians for any work above a defined threshold. Your touring package may need local augmentation even if gear travels.
- **Client vs. exhibitor vs. production classification** — Trade shows, corporate general sessions, and branded activations can fall under different rate tables and minimum calls. Confirm how the venue classifies your program before you budget labor.

Ask for the venue's labor rules summary in writing during the RFP stage — not a verbal "we'll figure it out at load-in." Properties that cannot produce a clear jurisdiction document are telling you something about advance planning.

## Questions to ask the venue before you sign

Bring these to the sales manager, and copy your production partner on the answers. Discrepancies between venue sales and the dock office are common; get labor specifics in the contract exhibit, not the banquet packet.

1. **Which trades are required for our scope?** — Rigging, power tie-in, scenic load-in, carpet and drape, fork lift, spot ops. List every discipline your show touches.
2. **What are the minimum crew calls and shift lengths?** — Four-hour minimums, eight-hour straight time, and turnaround rules between calls vary by local. A "half-day load-in" may still trigger a full call.
3. **Who can touch what?** — Can your A1 patch your console, or does an electrician need to be present? Can your LED tech climb a genie lift, or does a rigger need to fly every tile? These boundaries define how many hands you need and how fast load-in moves.
4. **What are the dock and freight elevator rules?** — Appointment windows, exclusive use fees, weight limits, and whether the union clock starts at the first truck arrival or first box on the dock.
5. **What triggers overtime and meal penalties?** — Sixth-day premiums, missed meal breaks, and show-day extensions add up faster than gear rental. Know the triggers before you lock the run-of-show.
6. **Is there a preferred rigging vendor or house AV requirement?** — Some venues mandate house labor for any overhead work regardless of who owns the gear.
7. **How are change orders handled on show day?** — Who authorizes extra crew, and what is the sign-off path when load-in runs long and you need a second shift?

Document answers in your venue comparison spreadsheet. Two ballrooms with identical room rates and wildly different labor minimums are not the same total cost.

## Map labor to your production scope

Union labor is not one line item — it is a set of trades tied to specific tasks. Your production brief should mirror how the venue bills, so your vendor can bid crew augmentation accurately instead of discovering gaps at the dock.

Typical trade breakdown for a corporate general session:

- **Riggers** — Overhead truss, flown LED or projection, motors, chain hoists, and any work above a defined height threshold. Ground-supported LED may still require riggers for cable management or safety oversight depending on local rules.
- **Electricians** — Power distribution tie-in, distro placement, and sometimes any cable run above a certain amperage or distance. Confirm whether your vendor's distro can be patched by your team or requires a licensed house electrician standing by.
- **Stagehands / carpenters** — Deck build, scenic load-in, drape and masking, rolling cases, and strike. Some venues separate carpenters from general stagehands; both may have minimum calls.
- **Spot operators / AV labor** — Follow spots, house camera operators, or dedicated AV labor when house systems are involved. Clarify whether your camera crew can operate house feeds or if union operators are mandatory.
- **Forklift / rigging support** — Heavy LED frames, ground-stack towers, and freight movement on the dock. Forklift operators often carry their own minimum call separate from stagehands.

Build a labor map against your show file: what travels with your vendor, what the venue supplies, and where the handoff happens. A show that needs twelve riggers because nobody confirmed ceiling height and fly points during advance is a planning failure, not a production surprise.

## Load-in windows, rehearsal, and the schedule math

Union clocks do not pause for your CEO rehearsal block. Load-in windows, strike deadlines, and holdover rules should drive your production timeline — not the other way around.

Schedule assumptions to lock in your brief:

- **Dock appointment vs. soft arrival** — If the venue assigns a two-hour dock slot, your entire load-in plan must fit inside it or you book a second window at second-window rates.
- **Parallel vs. sequential trades** — Electricians cannot tie in until riggers confirm points; stagehands cannot deck until power is live. Sequential dependencies eat calendar time even when crew counts look adequate on paper.
- **Rehearsal protection** — Rehearsal is the first thing compressed when load-in runs long. If union rules cap daily hours or mandate turnaround, build rehearsal as a named block with labor cost included — not "if we have time."
- **Show-day labor extensions** — General sessions that run long trigger overtime on crew still on clock for strike prep. Know whether your labor bid includes a show-day buffer or bills hourly after hour eight.
- **Strike and out-time penalties** — Missing the contracted out-time can trigger overnight crew rates or facility penalties. Align strike start with catering breakdown and audience egress, not "whenever the awards end."

Share the venue labor summary with your production partner before they bid. Vendors who price labor from a template room in a non-union market will underbid union properties every time — and make up the gap in change orders or cut rehearsal.

## Contract language and budget line items

Labor belongs in three places: the venue contract exhibit, the production SOW, and your internal budget with explicit contingency.

What to require in writing:

- **Named labor rates and minimum calls** by trade — not "prevailing union rates apply."
- **Estimated crew counts** for your documented scope — rigging plot, power plan, deck dimensions, and scenic inventory attached as exhibits.
- **Overtime and penalty triggers** with worked examples (e.g., load-in exceeding six hours, show running past 10 PM).
- **Change-order authorization** — who on your team can approve extra crew on show day, and the hourly rate cap.
- **Excluded vs. included tasks** — explicitly state what your production vendor crew performs versus what requires union labor.

Budget 10–15% contingency on union venues for first-time properties or complex rigging scopes. Repeat annuals at the same convention center can tighten that — but only if last year's debrief captured actual labor hours versus estimate.

## From the floor: the clock that started at parking

A corporate general session booked a convention center ballroom with a 6 AM dock appointment and a 2 PM rehearsal hard start. Sales confirmed the window. Nobody copied the labor steward on the production schedule.

The first truck arrived at 5:47 AM. The steward started the rigging call at 5:47 AM. The second truck — carrying the LED processor rack the riggers needed before they could confirm fly points — was stuck in morning traffic and arrived at 6:38 AM. The four-hour minimum still applied. Rehearsal started at 3:20 PM. The CEO walk-through got twelve minutes.

The following year, the brief listed sequential dock times, named which truck carried rigging-critical freight, and required the venue labor contact on the advance call. Same ballroom. Same union local. Different paperwork.

Union labor is predictable once you ask the right questions early and put the answers where finance, events, and production all read them. Treat jurisdiction, minimum calls, and who-can-touch-what as venue selection criteria — not production day surprises.
