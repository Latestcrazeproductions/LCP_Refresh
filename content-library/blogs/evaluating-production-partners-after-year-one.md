The first contract year with a production partner is when patterns replace pitches. You have load-in schedules, rehearsal cuts, change orders, capture deliverables, and at least one show report that either matches the brief or explains why it does not. Year one is not the moment to ask whether everyone liked working together. It is the moment to decide whether this partner earns renewal, a competitive rebid, or a split scope where they keep general session and lose touring markets. This guide defines what to measure after twelve months, how to separate one bad venue from a systemic miss, and how partner evaluation should change next year's production standards — not just next year's PO.

## What year-one performance actually proves

After year one, you are not grading charisma at the pre-con. You are grading whether the partner operationalized the standards you documented before signature — and whether their misses predict year-two risk.

Strong year-one evidence looks like this:

- **Brief fidelity across shows** — The partner restated your outcome in writing before each quote, flagged assumptions before load-in, and debriefed against the same brief after load-out. If year-one debriefs keep inventing new success criteria, you were buying labor, not a production program.
- **Advance discipline that travels** — Room drawings reviewed against your content, RF plans shared with venue and show management, load-in schedules confirmed with dock rules before trucks roll. One perfect advance and two "we'll figure it out on site" markets is a bench-depth signal, not bad luck.
- **Rehearsal protection on flagship segments** — Named checkpoints completed before doors: farthest-seat slide legibility, wireless walk-through on CEO and demo segments, stream path verification when hybrid mattered. Partners who consistently trade rehearsal for dock convenience score well on invoice totals and poorly on message fidelity.
- **Honest incident reporting** — Near-misses logged with root cause, not buried in loading-dock conversation. A partner who flags a switcher path risk at rehearsal close is managing your program. A partner who mentions it only when marketing rejects footage is managing their renewal conversation.
- **Content handoff that marketing can use** — Files on timeline, program audio isolated, framing that matches the capture scope in the brief. Year one with two of eight planned assets shipped late is a column on your scorecard, not a separate negotiation with the edit bay.

Weak year-one evidence is equally predictable: identical bids with different crew quality market to market, change orders that appear only after you are committed on site, post-show summaries that read like thank-you notes, and survey scores that say "AV was fine" while clarity questions fail on the general session.

If you did not capture any of the above during year one, your evaluation will default to relationship and price — which is how the same compressed rehearsal survives three budget cycles.

## Renewal, rebid, or split scope: decision criteria

After year one, most teams default to renewal because switching feels expensive. That is only true if you ignore the cost of repeating the same misses. Use explicit criteria before the calendar forces a rushed extension.

**Renew with confidence when:**

- Message fidelity and reliability scored at or above your threshold on every flagship show — with written evidence, not planner memory.
- Content leverage met marketing's signed capture standard on at least eighty percent of planned assets without emergency re-shoots or apology edits.
- Change orders stayed inside the band you defined in the contract — and each one tied to a documented client scope change, not an advance gap the partner should have caught.
- The same technical leadership (or named backup) showed up on advance and rehearsal for high-stakes segments, including multi-market legs where crew swaps are common.
- Post-show debriefs happened within forty-eight hours and produced action items that appeared in the next show's brief — proof the partner treats year two as improvement, not repetition.

**Rebid or replace when:**

- The same column on your [vendor scorecard](/blog/vendor-scorecards-for-event-production) scored below threshold twice — especially rehearsal discipline, brief adherence, or content handoff.
- Market-to-market variance is unexplained: strong general session in one city, different crew with no show file in another. That is not a regional quirk; it is a governance failure.
- Escalation paths failed when hybrid drift, RF density, or union dock surprises required a named decision-maker on site — and nobody with authority appeared within the window your brief defined.
- Finance sees year-one production spend rise while content utilization flatlines. The partner is not failing visibly on show day; they are failing the executive outcome production was funded to protect.
- The partner resists your scorecard, remediation plans, or documented standards — which tells you year two will be negotiated under load-in pressure again.

**Split scope when:**

- The partner excels at general session design and show calling but consistently under-delivers on capture, streaming, or breakout scaling.
- You need a primary partner for flagship cities and vetted locals elsewhere — but only if the primary owns file transfer, advance review, and rehearsal standards for every market. Split scope without a single source of truth is two vendors and zero accountability.

Document the decision in writing with the criteria you used. Leadership should not hear "we renewed because they're easy to work with" when the scorecard said otherwise.

## Questions to bring to the year-one review

Schedule a year-one review with marketing, events, finance (when spend is material), and the partner's account lead plus technical director — not sales alone. Bring your scorecard roll-up, debrief action items, content utilization report, and change-order log. Ask questions that force specifics:

1. **Which failures were preventable with the budget and brief we had?** Separates under-briefing from under-funding. Vague answers here predict vague year-two quotes.
2. **Where did we assume instead of document — and what did it cost?** Surfaces backup handheld placement, stream delay tolerance, graphics safe zones for the actual LED spec, who can call a hold on the CEO segment.
3. **What would you change if we repeated the same venue and format tomorrow?** Tests honesty beyond "client changed scope at the last minute."
4. **Which markets or show types did your bench handle well versus where you augmented with subs who never saw the show file?** Multi-market programs live or die on this answer.
5. **What single standard added to our pre-event brief would have the highest impact on year two?** Gives you one RFP addition instead of a twelve-page wish list.
6. **How do you report against our scorecard columns after load-out — and who signs it on your side?** Partners who cannot describe their process will not improve yours.

Require written follow-up within one week. Verbal commitments made in the year-one review should appear as named checkpoints in the renewal SOW or the rebid RFP — not as promises in the account manager's inbox.

## Business outcomes when year-one evaluation is disciplined

Evaluating production partners after year one is not procurement theater. Programs that score consistently and act on the results see executive outcomes that gear lists never produce.

When year-one review ties to scorecards, debriefs, and sourcing decisions:

- **Renewal conversations get defensible** — You extend because message fidelity and content leverage trended up across shows — not because switching vendors feels inconvenient in Q4.
- **Rebid scope gets sharper** — Finalists compete on brief adherence and rehearsal discipline, not identical panel counts with hidden assumptions. Price stays in the room; it stops being the only column.
- **Budget requests map to documented misses** — "We need show-caller coverage" lands when it follows a logged schedule variance on the flagship segment, not when it is a planner preference.
- **Multi-market consistency becomes measurable** — Program-level roll-ups show where the partner owns quality versus where subs dropped advance standards. That pattern drives split-scope decisions or mandatory TD travel — with finance backing the math.
- **Marketing trust in capture rises** — When year-one evaluation includes content leverage, production stops being the team that "made the room look good" and becomes the team that feeds the campaign calendar.
- **Vendor bench depth is visible before a crisis** — You know which partner handles hybrid failover and which one needs your events lead in every RF coordination call — before the CEO walk-on.

Track trends across the full year, not one heroic save on show day. A partner who rescues a keynote after a shortened rehearsal still owes you an honest score on rehearsal discipline. Heroics are not a renewal strategy.

## From the floor: the renewal deck that skipped the scorecard

An events team finished year one with the same partner on a four-market partner summit. Finance had the invoices — four bids within six percent of each other, change orders clustered in two markets. Marketing had the asset count — five of eleven planned clips published on time, the rest delayed because program audio carried room mic bleed and presenter cameras hunted focus on the panel segments.

The year-one review deck led with attendee satisfaction and a photo of the LED wall from market one. Nobody opened the scorecard roll-up because "we never formalized scoring after the first show." The debrief from market three — where rehearsal was cut to protect a dock schedule the vendor had never confirmed — lived in a text thread, not in the action register.

The recommendation was automatic renewal with a three percent uplift. Marketing objected in the hallway, not in the meeting. Year two opened with the same capture scope language and no isolated program mix requirement. Market two of year two repeated the audio problem in a different ballroom with a different LED safe zone mistake — same partner, same missing advance review, no remediation plan from the review nobody documented.

The partner was not the villain. The program had year-one data and no decision criteria — so relationship and invoice variance decided the contract, and the expensive columns (content leverage, brief adherence) stayed invisible until the edit bay failed again.

Score the year before you renew. Bring the questions. Let the answers change the brief — or change the vendor.
