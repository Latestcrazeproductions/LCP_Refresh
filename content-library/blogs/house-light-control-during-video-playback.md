Video playback segments only work when the room goes dark enough for the screen to read — and when someone actually executes that fade on time. House light control during video rolls is one of the most common scope gaps on corporate general sessions: the venue owns the dimmer rack, your production vendor programmed the lighting looks, and the show caller is counting on a blackout that nobody agreed to run.

This guide covers who should own house dimmers during playback, what to lock in the brief before load-in, and how to split control between venue, lighting, and show calling without a half-lit ballroom fighting your content.

## Why house lights matter more during video than during slides

Slide-driven keynotes tolerate moderate house levels. The projector or LED wall is bright; presenters are lit separately. Full-frame video — sizzle reels, customer stories, cinematic openers — needs contrast. Ambient spill from house cans, soffits, and aisle lights bleeds into the screen, crushes shadow detail on camera, and reads as amateur on IMAG.

Three variables move together during every video cue:

- **House level** — percentage or preset name on architectural dimmers, not just "go black" on stage lighting.
- **Stage and presenter lighting** — whether talent stays in a hold, a silhouette preset, or full blackout with no spill on the deck.
- **Screen and IMAG path** — switcher on program video, shaders expecting a stable exposure, delay screens matching the main feed.

If house dimmers sit with the venue electrician while your LD only controls the truss package, video playback becomes a negotiation in real time unless you assign ownership upfront.

## Ownership model: venue, LD, show caller, and playback

There is no universal rule — it depends on the building — but every successful show names one person who executes the house fade and one person who calls it.

Use this split as a starting point for your pre-con:

**Venue / house AV (when they retain dimmer access)**

- Executes fades on house architectural circuits the production vendor cannot patch.
- Provides a contact who can recall venue presets or bypass local wall stations during the show.
- Confirms whether house lights are on a separate control system from your production dimmers.

**Production lighting director**

- Owns stage lighting presets for video holds — no stray front light on an empty deck, no followspot hunting during a full-frame roll.
- Programs and triggers looks on gear under production control; coordinates timing with the TD on IMAG black or logo holds between clips.
- Documents which house levels the show needs and who must hit them (often the LD on a shared net, sometimes a venue tech on intercom).

**Show caller**

- Calls the video cue and the associated lighting state in one breath — "Roll hero reel, house out, stage black, video to program."
- Does not assume the venue tech is listening unless they are on the same intercom channel with an assigned button.

**Playback / TD**

- Confirms content is cued and program is switched before house drops — avoids a dark room staring at a desktop mirror.
- Holds a agreed "abort" path if house will not drop (skip video, extend slide, or accept a brighter house compromise).

Gray zone to kill in writing: "House will go dark for video." Dark for whom? Stage only, or entire ballroom including registration spill from the pre-function?

## House levels and presets to spec before load-in

Vague language produces vague rooms. Replace "blackout for video" with named targets both teams can rehearse.

**Typical house targets by segment type**

- **Full-frame cinematic video (no presenter on stage)** — house at 0–5% or full off on audience cans; aisle and egress per fire marshal, not per creative.
- **Video with voice-over, empty stage** — house 0–10%; confirm exit signs and path lighting stay code-compliant without lighting the screen wall.
- **Video under presenter (IMAG box or picture-in-picture)** — house may stay 15–30% for note-taking; stage key reduced, not house randomly left at walk-in levels.
- **Walk-in / exit** — named preset (e.g. "House 70") that never requires the LD to rebuild from scratch between segments.

**Handoff checklist — bring this to venue and production on one call**

1. **Single owner for house fade on video cues** — name the person and their intercom channel.
2. **Preset map** — list venue preset numbers or DMX submaster labels next to run-of-show segments.
3. **Minimum egress lighting** — what stays on, who locks it, and whether it hits the screen surface.
4. **Wall station and override policy** — who disables local dimmers in the ballroom during general session.
5. **Rehearsal proof** — full video cue with house fade in cue-to-cue, not a verbal "we'll try it in show."
6. **Failure mode** — if house will not drop below 20%, who decides to rebalance screen brightness or cut the segment.

## Questions for your venue and lighting vendor

References beat promises. Ask both parties the same questions and compare answers.

1. **"Who physically pushes the house fade for our opening video — your tech, our LD, or a shared control net?"**
2. **"Are house dimmers on the same console as the stage plot, and if not, how do you sync cues?"**
3. **"What house level did your last general session in this room use for a three-minute reel, and what did IMAG look like?"**
4. **"If a wall station gets bumped mid-show, who gets the call and how fast can we restore the show preset?"**
5. **"Does your stream shader expect us to hold a house level between clips, or can we go full black between every file?"**

If answers diverge, fix the org chart before fixturing — not at doors.

## From the floor: the fade that was "already handled"

A general session once opened with a two-minute brand film the CMO had signed off in a darkened screening room. Rehearsal morning, the LD ran stage blacks beautifully. Nobody told the house electrician the reel was tonight's first cue — he was told only that "the outside vendor has lighting."

Doors open, walk-in at seventy percent. Show caller: roll video, house out. Stage went black on cue. The house cans over the first ten rows stayed at forty because the venue preset was tied to a wall station nobody had locked. On IMAG, the reel looked fine. In the room, it looked like a laptop with the brightness turned up. The CEO watched half the opening from the wings asking if the file was wrong.

The fix was boring and perfect: one line in the run-of-show, one venue tech on channel two, one preset labeled `VID-FULL`, tested with butts in seats. The content was never the problem. The dimmer had an owner; it just was not the owner anyone called.
