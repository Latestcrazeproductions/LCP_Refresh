Ground-supported and flown LED walls solve the same content problem with different physics. The choice comes down to what the venue allows, how the room is shaped, and whether the wall sits behind a stage deck or hangs as its own visual plane. Lock that before pixel pitch or panel count — structure drives everything else.

This guide covers when each mounting approach fits corporate ballrooms and general sessions, what to verify in the venue contract, and how to spec the decision in an RFP without defaulting to whatever looked best in a render.

## What each mounting type actually means

**Ground-supported** LED walls sit on a truss or ground-stack frame anchored to the stage deck or floor. Modules stack vertically from the floor up; lateral stability comes from the base structure, ballast, or ties to the deck. Load travels through the stage or floor — not through ceiling rigging points.

**Flown** LED walls hang from motor points, chain hoists, or fixed rigging in the ceiling structure. Weight and moment load go to rated rigging points; the wall can float above the stage lip, clear scenic, or position independently of the deck footprint.

Both can deliver the same pixel pitch and brightness. The production difference is load path, sight-line geometry, load-in sequence, and what the venue will sign off on in writing.

## When ground-supported wins

Corporate hotel ballrooms and convention breakouts default to ground support for good reason: fewer rigging approvals, predictable load-in, and a wall that integrates with a stage deck the way most general sessions are already designed.

Choose ground-supported when:

- **The venue restricts or forbids flown LED** — Many hotel contracts cap rigging weight, ban motor loads over the audience, or require union riggers for any overhead hang. Ground support keeps the wall in the stage footprint.
- **You have a defined stage deck** — Presenter area, lectern, confidence monitors, and a backdrop wall share one engineered platform. Cable paths run along the deck; processors sit downstage or in a tech booth with short home runs.
- **Ceiling height is tight** — A 12–14 foot ballroom ceiling leaves little room to fly a 10-foot wall above trim, lighting, and a scenic header. Ground support uses the vertical space you actually have.
- **Load-in access is constrained** — Freight elevators, no dock, or a single overnight window favor modular stacks that build from the floor without lift equipment over seats.
- **The show tours multiple rooms** — Ground-stack systems repackage faster than a flown plot that must be re-engineered to different rigging maps city to city.
- **You need the wall flush to the deck** — Product demos, panel discussions, and awards with people crossing downstage expect a continuous backdrop plane, not a floating canvas with a visible gap at the floor line.

Ground support is not "less professional." It is the correct engineering path when the room, schedule, or venue policy makes overhead load the wrong variable to fight.

## When flown wins

Flown walls earn their rigging hours when the room geometry or creative layout needs the canvas off the deck — or when ground support would eat staging depth you cannot spare.

Choose flown when:

- **Sight lines demand height without sacrificing deck depth** — A shallow stage with a deep audience needs the wall elevated so row one sees content above the presenters' heads, not blocked by a tall ground stack sitting on a 8-foot-deep deck.
- **The wall is not tied to the stage** — Center-room installs, delay screens over the audience mid-point, or a hero canvas upstage of a minimal presentation zone often have no deck to anchor against.
- **Scenic wants a clean floor line** — No legs, no base truss in the shot, no gap between wall and turntable. Flying the wall removes ground-structure clutter from camera frames and IMAG.
- **Rigging points are verified and available** — Convention centers with published point maps and in-house riggers can fly faster than building a 30-foot-wide ground support that needs ballast and deck reinforcement.
- **Weight on the deck is the constraint** — Older stages, portable decks, or ballrooms with low point loads may accept a flown wall's load in the ceiling easier than a ground stack concentrated on four feet of decking.
- **You need precise trim height** — Motor control lets you fine-tune wall height during focus to clear a lighting trim or align with a projection surface — adjustments that ground stacks handle with blocking and shims.

Flown is not automatic in a ballroom. It is the right call when rigging is confirmed early and the design actually uses the vertical freedom.

## Side-by-side decision checklist

Run your room through this before you attach a mounting type to the RFP. Count the wins in each column; a clear majority usually tells you which path to spec first.

| Factor | Ground-supported | Flown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Venue rigging policy | Ground support or limited overhead OK | Rated points, motors, and rigger labor confirmed |
| Stage integration | Wall is the backdrop to a deck | Wall is independent of deck footprint |
| Ceiling / trim height | Low to moderate; wall height capped by room | Adequate fly height above lighting and scenic |
| Load-in window | Single crew, no lifts over seats | Rigging crew and lift access scheduled |
| Sight lines (front rows) | Wall starts at deck; may need setback | Wall elevated; presenters below content plane |
| Tour / multi-city | Same stack builds in varied rooms | Requires rigging survey per venue |
| Camera / IMAG | Base structure visible unless masked | Clean floor line; fewer legs in frame |
| Deck weight limits | Must verify point load on deck | Load to ceiling structure |

When the checklist splits evenly, hybrid appears more often than planners expect: a ground-supported center wall with flown delay screens, or a modest flown hero canvas plus ground-supported side wings for IMAG. Spec each surface's mounting type independently.

## Questions for the venue and your production partner

These belong in the advance packet — not in the email chain the night before load-in.

- **Written rigging policy** — Are flown LED walls permitted? Who provides rigging labor (in-house, union, your vendor)? What documentation do they require (rigging plot, stamped calcs, insurance riders)?
- **Point load and weight limits** — For flown: per-point capacity, total load, and whether motors over the audience are allowed. For ground support: deck point load, maximum stack height, and ballast rules on finished floor.
- **Ceiling obstructions** — Sprinklers, chandeliers, acoustic clouds, and soffits that set maximum trim height or force a setback from centerline.
- **Sight-line verification** — Who produces a section view showing wall height versus first row, balcony lip, and camera platform? A napkin sketch beats assumptions.
- **Load-in path** — Dock to room, elevator dimensions, and whether ground stacks can be built in place or must be pre-rigged and flown in sections (some venues reverse the usual order).
- **Power and cable routing** — Home run distance from wall to processor rack; floor channels versus overhead cable paths; who patches around fire lanes.
- **Strike timeline** — Ground stacks often strike after scenic; flown walls may need to come out before deck load-out. Sequence affects labor and overtime.

Answers should land in the production brief with a named mounting type per wall surface. "We'll decide on site" is how a flown spec becomes a ground stack at midnight — with a different quote, different sight lines, and a rehearsal block lost to structural debate.

## What to put in the RFP

Do not assume vendors will propose the right mounting approach for your room. State it, with acceptance criteria tied to the format.

- **Mounting type per wall** — Ground-supported on deck, flown from specified points, or hybrid with a diagram showing each surface.
- **Maximum footprint** — Deck depth consumed by wall structure, presenter zone, and camera cross-shoot clearance.
- **Trim height range** — Minimum and maximum top of wall relative to finished floor; flown walls include motor travel if applicable.
- **Structural documentation** — Ground support: ballast plan and deck load calculation. Flown: rigging plot with point map and total suspended weight.
- **Load-in / strike sequence** — Hours required, crew size, and dependencies on scenic and lighting.
- **Failover** — Processor redundancy and module swap access without dismantling the entire structure (ground stacks with rear service lanes vs flown walls with catwalk access).

## From the floor: the rigging plot that assumed the wrong ceiling

A general session arrived with a flown 36-foot wall in the production brief because that was the reference photo from a convention center. The actual venue was a hotel ballroom with a 13-foot ceiling, three chandeliers in the trim path, and a contract clause that prohibited motor loads over seated guests. The rigging vendor discovered this at the pre-con, not at booking.

The working solution was a ground-supported center wall on a 24-inch riser — same pixel pitch, narrower width, integrated with the stage deck — plus two modest flown side screens on the only rated points over the stage lip, not the audience. Same content pipeline, different load paths. The show looked intentional; the original spec would not have cleared the ceiling if it had been built as drawn.

Mounting type is a venue decision dressed as a creative one. Confirm structure, sight lines, and contract language before you fall in love with a floating canvas.
