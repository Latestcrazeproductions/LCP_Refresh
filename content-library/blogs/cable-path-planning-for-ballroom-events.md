Ballroom shows run on power, signal, and comms — and every one of those runs wants the shortest path across a floor banquet still treats as a finished surface. Cable path planning is where production reality meets hotel ops, ADA expectations, and a fire marshal who does not care that your distro landed late. If paths live only on a CAD layer nobody prints for the house, you get mats at the last minute, ramps that do not match chair legs, and egress lanes that shrink every time someone adds a monitor world extension.

This guide covers what to document before load-in: primary and backup cable routes, ramp and mat tiers for guest and crew traffic, fire marshal and egress non-negotiables, and the venue contract asks that keep paths legal after the first coffee break.

## What belongs on a cable path plan (not just a gear list)

A gear list tells you what to ship. A cable path plan tells you where each run may cross the floor, who may cross it during show hours, and how you protect the surface when the room flips from general session to gala. Build it from the same floor plan you use for staging — with layers for audience seating, FOH, camera positions, and strike corridors.

Minimum elements to finalize in advance and share with venue ops, production, and safety:

1. **Primary paths** — From dock or storage to stage, FOH, LED processor rack, camera positions, and stream table. Mark width (minimum 36 inches for a shared crew lane; wider if carts move on the same path).
2. **Crossover points** — Where runs pass from stage left to stage right, under air walls, or through pre-function into the ballroom. Each crossover gets a named protection method (mat, ramp, overhead if permitted).
3. **Traffic classification** — Which segments are crew-only after doors open vs. which guest paths must stay flat, covered, and ADA-compliant. Do not mix them on one sketch without labels.
4. **Power vs. signal separation** — Parallel runs are fine; crossing AC distro with sensitive SDI or Dante paths without shielding or distance is a troubleshooting tax. Note separation on the plan, not in a hallway conversation at 5 a.m.
5. **Overnight and flip windows** — When paths may be broken for banquet reset, who re-lays mats, and whether cables stay dressed under tables or must fully clear the floor.
6. **Single owner** — One show role (often production PM or lead tech) signs off on path changes during the event. Ad hoc reroutes are how egress gaps appear mid-show.

Send the plan to the venue with enough lead time for engineering and safety review — not the night before when the only answer left is “we’ll tape it.”

## Ramps, mats, and floor protection tiers

Hotels care about carpet, vinyl, and liability. Production cares about rolling cases and sub-snakes that cannot live in the ceiling. Mats and ramps are the compromise — if they are spec’d early enough to order the right profile and quantity.

Use three tiers so advance conversations stay concrete:

- **Tier 1 — Guest egress paths** — ADA-aligned ramps (slope, landings, edge treatment), solid-cover cable protectors rated for foot traffic, high-visibility edging where paths cross attendee routes, and no hump higher than local code allows for public walkways. These paths survive fire marshal walkthrough and the first rush to bar tables.
- **Tier 2 — Crew and talent crossovers** — Heavier-duty covers where carts and road cases roll; wider channels for multi-cable bundles; gaffer-tape is for labeling, not structural cover on a public lane. Talent paths from green room to stage use Tier 1 rules even if only VIPs walk them — staff and security will use the same lane.
- **Tier 3 — Build and strike only** — Temporary runs, bare ramp boards, and uncapped bundles allowed only when the room is closed to guests. Strike these before any soft opening, sponsor walk-through, or rehearsal with executives on the floor.

Match mat width to chair geometry. Banquet setups with tight row spacing turn a 24-inch cover into a toe stubber for every third seat. If the room stays in classroom or chevron during production days, note which rows get removed before paths go down — and who orders that move (house vs. production).

Ask your production partner to quote protection explicitly. “Includes mats” without quantity, tier, or length is how you rent twelve feet of cover for eighty feet of run.

## Fire marshal, egress, and what overrides your plot

In ballrooms, the fire marshal (or local AHJ) treats aisles and exits as show-critical infrastructure — not negotiable after the fact. Cable paths that narrow exit width, block doors, or bridge exits without listed covers fail walkthrough and force a rebuild under clock pressure.

Non-negotiables to design around from the first sketch:

- **Exit door clearance** — No cable bundles, distro, or ramp within the door swing arc or the required clear width at the threshold. Plan tie-ins so heavy cable does not have to exit through the same door as the audience.
- **Common path of travel** — Aisles marked on the fire plan stay aisles during show. You cannot reclaim them for FOH extensions or camera track without a revised seating layout and approval.
- **Listed covers on rated paths** — Many jurisdictions require UL-listed or locally accepted cable protectors on egress routes; gaffer-built plywood channels are not a substitute on public paths.
- **Flame spread and trip hazard** — Loose carpet edges, curled mat corners, and unsecured ramps fail for trip hazard before they fail for fire code. Dress ends and tape policy should be in the safety brief, not improvised by whoever has gaffer left on the belt.
- **Load-in vs. occupied load** — Rules tighten when the room is open to guests. A path that passed at 10 a.m. with the room empty may fail at 5 p.m. with tables in place.

Schedule the marshal walkthrough (or venue safety review) after paths are dressed for show conditions — chairs in, lights dim, worst-case crowd — not on an empty shell with cables taped to a bare floor.

## Contract and advance asks that keep paths legal

Cable paths fail when the venue contract is silent on who protects the floor, who moves chairs for a crossover, and who pays when the marshal stops load-in until paths are rebuilt.

Put these in the venue agreement or signed advance — not only in the production rider:

- **Named path review meeting** — Date, attendees (hotel ops, production lead, safety if available), and deliverable: signed sketch or redlined floor plan.
- **Floor protection standards** — Who supplies Tier 1 covers, minimum specs, and replacement cost if hotel-owned carpet is damaged under production paths.
- **Chair and table moves** — Who clears rows for cable runs, timing relative to banquet, and whether production may not dress paths until house reset completes.
- **Egress preservation** — Language that production will maintain approved aisle widths; venue provides the certified seating layout fire review used as the baseline.
- **Overnight security** — Paths left dressed overnight are tampered with by cleaning, banquet, or the random photo shoot. Define who may touch mats and who is called if paths are disturbed.

Align path planning with [stage production scope](/services/stage) — deck height, wing width, and processor rack location drive where bundles concentrate. If power tie-ins sit far from FOH, your path plan is also a power plan; coordinate both in the same advance packet.

## From the floor: the marshal and the sub-snake

A general session had one acceptable crossover from stage right to FOH — forty feet through a banquet aisle that would stay open for a sponsor lunch on the same day. Production dressed it with heavy crew covers overnight. At 11 a.m., catering shifted six tables six inches to fit linen storage, pinching the aisle without telling anyone. The sub-snake still worked. The listed guest path did not.

The fire marshal measured before lunch service, not before breakfast load-in. Rebuild took forty minutes, mostly unrolling mats and re-centering a ramp while executives watched from the pre-function. Nobody got fined; everyone lost the rehearsal buffer.

The lesson was not dramatic — it was that the path plan had a line on paper and no owner on the floor when banquet moved furniture. After that, crossover paths got taped on the carpet with hotel sign-off, and the run-of-show listed who to call if a table moved. Mats are boring until they are the only thing between you and a hold on doors.
