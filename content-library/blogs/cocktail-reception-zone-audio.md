Cocktail reception audio and ballroom program audio are two shows sharing one building. Reception wants energy and movement music; the ballroom needs speech intelligibility, tighter dynamics, and a mix that does not compete with a foyer that never fully closes. When both run through one untreated PA feed, FOH chases the wrong problem all night.

This guide covers what planners should lock before the floor plan is final: how to draw zone boundaries, what levels belong where, and how to rehearse the handoff when guests are still standing in the corridor during the first cue.

## Draw zones from guest flow, not from the CAD outline

Hotel ballrooms rarely end where the architecture says they end. Pre-function space, airwall pockets, registration tables, and a bar that sits half in the foyer and half in the ballroom all become listening zones whether audio design accounts for them or not.

Define zones in the production brief before equipment hits the dock:

- **Reception / pre-function** — Standing density, music-forward, no assumption that anyone is facing a screen or a stage.
- **Ballroom seated program** — Speech priority, defined listening area from the last occupied table to FOH, delay fills if the room is deep or under a balcony.
- **Transition corridor** — The path between zones; treat it as its own problem, not as "overflow" that magically inherits ballroom tuning.
- **Adjacent rooms on airwalls** — Breakouts, press areas, and sponsor lounges that share a hard wall with either zone; bleed is bidirectional.

Mark each zone on the advance packet with who owns levels (reception DJ vs. production FOH), where speakers aim, and which doors stay open during which segment. Vague "we'll turn it down for the program" language is how reception music washes the first presenter walk-on.

## Levels, content, and what each zone is allowed to do

Reception and ballroom need different loudness targets and different content rules. Same speaker brand does not mean same mix philosophy.

Use these starting targets in your brief — adjust with your audio lead for room acoustics, not for sponsor preference:

- **Reception / standing** — Music bed loud enough to register as "event energy" at a normal speaking volume between guests; typically higher average SPL than seated dinner, with limiter discipline so peaks do not spike into the ballroom when doors open.
- **Ballroom seated** — Speech intelligibility at the back row without straining; music beds ducked under VOG and presenter mics; walk-on tracks timed to end before lavs open.
- **Corridor / transition** — Often 6–10 dB below reception peak at the ballroom doors; if guests cannot hear the reception vibe, they drift late — if they hear it at program levels, the emcee fights the foyer.
- **Program-start handoff** — Written cue: who fades reception, who opens ballroom doors, whether reception stays live at reduced level or switches to ambient-only playback.

Separate playback sources help more than separate volume knobs. Reception music from a DJ controller or dedicated playback laptop should not share a bus with ballroom VOG unless DSP routing is documented. A single auxiliary send turned up for cocktail hour is how keynote audio inherits last hour's playlist on the first slide.

## System design — splits that survive show day

Zone control on a line array, distributed speakers in the foyer, or a second small PA for pre-function each work when the signal path is drawn early. Retrofitting zones after the hang is when corridors get a spare speaker on a cable that nobody labeled.

Bring this checklist to your vendor walk-through:

- **DSP zones or matrices** — Named zones per room region; reception and ballroom on separate outputs with independent EQ and limiters, not one fader ride at FOH.
- **Door and airwall plan** — Which panels are open during reception vs. seated dinner vs. awards; SPL measured at the ballroom threshold with doors in realistic positions, not pinned open for load-in only.
- **Microphone isolation** — Presenter lavs and handhelds routed to ballroom zones only; reception DJ mic or announcement mic not bleeding into the keynote mix bus.
- **Background music source** — Licensed playlist path for reception; royalty-cleared walk-on and stings on a separate machine if marketing hands off tracks late.
- **RF and comms** — Show caller and audio lead can mute or fade reception without crossing into the DJ's mixer unknowingly; comms channel for "doors in two" and "reception down now."
- **Capture and stream** — If you broadcast the program, confirm the stream bus excludes reception playback; corridor bleed on a live feed is a common hybrid failure.

If house AV owns the ballroom and production owns reception — or the reverse — one name on the run of show must own master level authority across both. Split gear without split authority is how a foyer playlist lands on the CEO's segment.

## Rehearsal — test both room states, not just empty ballroom

Sound check in an empty ballroom with reception dark proves almost nothing. Block time to rehearse with bodies and doors where guests actually stand.

1. **Reception density** — Staff or volunteers at standing height in the foyer; verify reception levels and corridor spill at the ballroom doors with airwalls in show position.
2. **Seated layout** — Tables in place; ballroom trim for speech at the back row with reception still audible in the corridor at reduced level.
3. **Program open** — First VOG or presenter cue with reception at realistic "doors open" level; audio op should not need a frantic fader grab.
4. **Handoff cue** — Numbered cue on the run of show: reception fade, door close or airwall seal, ballroom walk-on; lighting and show caller on the same beat.
5. **Late-arrival path** — Someone walks in from reception during the first minute of program; confirm corridor bleed does not mask the opening line at the worst tables.

Document the handoff on the cue sheet with a department owner, not as "DJ will figure it out when we start."

## Questions for your audio vendor before you sign

- **Zone map** — Will you deliver a labeled zone diagram tied to DSP outputs, or a speaker plot with implied coverage only?
- **Who rides reception during program** — DJ, FOH engineer, or show caller via remote; what happens if that person is on a different floor?
- **House vs. production split** — If venue audio is in the ballroom, how does reception tie in without double amplification or phase issues at the doorway?
- **SPL measurement** — Will they measure at the ballroom threshold with reception active, and share targets in the advance?
- **Backup path** — If the reception playback machine fails, is there a documented switch that does not reset ballroom routing?
- **Strike and changeover** — When tables move from standing to seated, who re-trims zones and how long is that blocked on the timeline?

Answers belong in the brief. Dual-zone galas and conference dinners punish assumptions because two clocks run at once — F&B in the foyer and program on stage.

## From the floor: the doors that stayed open

A fundraising dinner ran clean line-array coverage in the ballroom and a competent DJ in pre-function. The mix in the room was fine. The airwall pocket by the bar stayed open for guest flow — reasonable for cocktail hour, never revisited when seats filled. The paddle-raise caller's first numbers competed with a reception track that was only "a little loud" at FOH, because FOH was inside the ballroom and the problem was three feet past the drape.

Reception dropped six decibels and the corridor speaker aim got a five-minute adjustment during dinner service — fixable, but not the look when the development director shot a glance at the back tables. The next advance listed doorway SPL, airwall positions per segment, and a numbered cue for who owns the fade when the program opens. Same hotel. Same DJ. Different zone discipline.

Cocktail reception zone audio works when zones are named early, levels are measured at the threshold, and the program handoff is a cue — not a hope. Spec it in the brief, rehearse with doors and bodies, and keep reception energy in the foyer instead of on the keynote bus.
