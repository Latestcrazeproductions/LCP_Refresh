A ballroom that looks perfect on a walkthrough can still ship a flown LED plot, a PA deployment, and a camera plan that do not fit the room. Site visits fail when the planner remembers ceiling height but not column spacing, or when the only photos in the advance folder are the catering setup from the sales tour. This checklist is what to measure and photograph on a venue visit so your production partner can bid and engineer against the room you actually booked — not the render on the hotel website.

Bring it on property with a tape measure, a laser if you have one, and permission to open service doors. The goal is a short report your AV vendor can use without scheduling a second trip because someone forgot the freight elevator depth.

## What to capture before you write the check

Sales tours optimize for natural light and wide angles. Production advances need the unglamorous angles: ceiling structure, breaker panels, dock turns, and the gap between a 12-foot air wall and the column nobody mentions until truss is on the truck. Photos without scale lie; measurements without context lie almost as well. Label each capture with room name, standing position, and time of day if windows affect glare on screens.

Walk the room twice if you can — once as a planner imagining flow and seating, once as a rigger and tech walking load-in paths backward from the dock. If house AV or an in-house electrician can join for thirty minutes, that meeting is worth more than a second hour of iPhone panos.

## Measurements production cannot guess from a floor plan

Venue CAD files are often banquet layouts, not production plots. Record these numbers on every visit and send them with your photo set. Round up to the nearest foot when you are between marks; production will sanity-check, but missing data is what forces change orders.

1. **Ceiling height** — Minimum clear height above the proposed stage footprint and above FOH; note drops, soffits, and acoustic clouds that reduce trim.
2. **Stage footprint** — Usable width and depth at finished floor, including obstructions (built-in stages, steps, pit covers) and maximum deck height the venue allows.
3. **Column grid and offsets** — Distance from downstage edge to first column row, column spacing, and diameter; columns determine sight lines and LED aspect ratios.
4. **Audience depth and ceiling rake** — Last row distance to screen or stage lip, and whether the ceiling slopes (common in ballrooms) so PA delay and screen height can be modeled.
5. **Door and path dimensions** — Ballroom entry width and height, air-wall opening width, freight elevator cab depth/width/door height, and any 90-degree turns on the load-in path.
6. **FOH and tech positions** — Distance from mix position to stage, sight line from FOH to screen, and whether FOH sits on riser, floor, or balcony rail.
7. **Power tie-in distance** — Approximate run from named panel locations to stage lip, FOH, and upstage processor position; note floor channels vs. overhead drops.
8. **Rigging reference** — If points are visible, distance between points over stage and trim at downstage edge; if not visible, ceiling type and who can provide an as-built rig plot later.

Write measurements on a printout of the room diagram or a tablet sketch with north/upstage marked. "Big room" is not a deliverable.

## Photograph these surfaces and choke points

Your camera roll should answer questions the production team will ask on the first advance call. Shoot in landscape, bracket exposures in dim service corridors, and capture nameplates and placards legibly.

- **Ceiling over stage and FOH** — Wide shot plus detail of structure type (exposed steel, lay-in tile, clouds), sprinklers, and any labeled rigging hardware.
- **Column lines from downstage** — One frame showing stage edge to columns, one from the middle of the audience toward the screen wall.
- **Screen or LED wall location** — Full wall width, power outlets within six feet, and sight line from row one and last row if seating is already set or taped.
- **Balcony or mezzanine** — Rail height, overhang depth, and whether delay fill or camera positions are viable underneath.
- **Power panels and disconnects** — Closed door (location context) and open door (breaker labels, amp ratings if readable).
- **Loading dock to ballroom path** — Dock bay, elevator threshold, hallway pinch points, and any steps or ramps with a case for scale.
- **House AV rack or head-end** — If the property runs breakouts or has patch panels you might tie into.
- **Windows and ambient light** — Daytime wash on the proposed screen wall; blackout capabilities (motorized shades, black drape track) if the show is IMAG-heavy.
- **Green room and presenter paths** — Route from holding to stage, including elevator capacity if talent moves between floors.

One floor plan photo with your shoe or tape measure visible in frame helps everyone agree the wide shot matches reality.

## Audio, lighting, and house rules worth noting in prose

Listen in the empty room: HVAC rumble, kitchen exhaust, and lobby bleed through pre-function doors show up in every lav recording. Note whether the hotel enforces noise curfews, union jurisdiction for rigging and power tie-ins, and whether outside production is allowed to use house points. Ask where wireless coordinators scan on show week and whether the property has a history of DECT or LED dimmer conflict — you may not get a spectrum print on visit one, but you should know who provides it.

Lighting for IMAG is not the same as lighting for dinner. Photograph existing fixtures above the stage zone and ask if house dimmers are shared with ballroom presets. If the room will flip from luncheon to general session, capture how long banquet needs the floor and whether lighting positions survive the flip.

Confirm Wi-Fi SSIDs only if hybrid or streaming is in scope; otherwise prioritize hardline paths and whether the venue permits bonded cellular or satellite at FOH. These answers belong in the same folder as rig photos so the planner and vendor are not reconciling two different site visits.

## From the floor: the site visit that only photographed the chandeliers

A planner walked a convention ballroom with sales and left with twelve beautiful photos of centerpieces and uplit drapery. Advance week, production asked for the freight elevator interior, the distance from the last air-wall panel to the loading corridor, and the breaker that fed the upstage wall. None of that was in the folder. The LED wall could fit the room — it could not fit the path without tipping panels vertical in an elevator that was four inches too shallow.

A second walk cost a day and a change order. The third event at the same property took forty-five minutes on site with a tape measure and a checklist: dock-to-stage photos first, ceiling second, power panels third. Sales still got their hero shot. Production got what they needed before the deposit cleared.

Venue site visits are cheap insurance against expensive load-in surprises. Measure the room, photograph the choke points, and send one package to production the same day you leave the property.
