Product launches live or die on timing: the reveal lands on the wrong beat, the demo video fires before the hardware is uncovered, or the house lights come up while the CEO is still mid-sentence. The run-of-show is where those failures get prevented — and most of the AV decisions that protect it belong on the planner's checklist, not buried in a vendor's internal spreadsheet.

This guide covers the AV milestones you should own from first draft through doors open: what to lock by when, what to bring to your production partner, and the rehearsal gates that catch sync problems before an audience sees them.

## Build the run-of-show around AV-critical beats first

Marketing teams write run-of-show documents around messaging arcs and speaker transitions. Production teams read them looking for cue points — where audio, video, lighting, and scenic have to move together. For a product launch, those beats are non-negotiable and they should appear in the document before anyone debates lunch timing.

AV-critical beats to define early:

- **Pre-show state** — house lighting level, walk-in loop or ambient content, mic status, and whether the LED wall shows holding slides or is dark.
- **Walk-on sequence** — music bed, VOG or live intro, presenter mic live point, and lighting preset change.
- **Reveal moment** — exact trigger (presenter line, button press, timed automation), what drops (scrim, curtain, case lid), and what fires simultaneously (video, lighting, SFX, IMAG switch).
- **Demo segments** — live product on stage vs. pre-recorded fallback, camera coverage plan, and who calls the switch if the live path fails.
- **Supporting content blocks** — sizzle reels, partner logos, spec slides; each needs an in/out cue and a defined audio path (program mix vs. VOG duck).
- **Transition resets** — how long between segments to reset scenic, reload content, or swap presenters without dead air.

If your run-of-show lists "Product Reveal — 10:22 AM" without sub-cues, your show caller is improvising the most important thirty seconds of the event. Break every major beat into technical sub-cues before you send the document to production.

## AV milestone timeline — what to lock and when

Product launch AV has hard dependencies: content renders at native wall resolution, scenic builds around rigging points, and rehearsal time is finite. Use this timeline to keep marketing, creative, and production aligned.

**Eight weeks out:**

- **Format locked** — seated keynote, theater-in-the-round, or demo lab walk-through; drives camera count, PA design, and whether IMAG is mandatory.
- **Reveal mechanism chosen** — scrim drop, lift platform, case open, wall content wipe; scenic and automation need this before shop drawings start.
- **Content aspect ratio locked** — native LED resolution and safe zones documented; motion and slide teams build to the same canvas.
- **Run-of-show v1** — all AV-critical beats with placeholder times; production can bid crew count and equipment against it.

**Four weeks out:**

- **Final speaker list and mic inventory** — every presenter, demo operator, and panelist assigned a wireless or wired path with backup.
- **Content pipeline defined** — who delivers final files, what format (ProRes, HAP, PNG sequences), and where they land on the media server.
- **Cue-to-cue scope agreed** — full run, abbreviated tech run, or segment-only; block calendar time accordingly.
- **RF and venue coordination started** — frequency scan date, union jurisdiction, and any broadcast restrictions if you're near a stadium or airport.

**One week out:**

- **Content locked** — no new renders after this date unless you accept untested files on show day.
- **Run-of-show vFinal** — times, sub-cues, contingency notes, and emergency holds documented; distributed to show caller, department heads, and client approver.
- **Rehearsal call sheet issued** — who must be on stage for each segment, not just "full rehearsal at 2 PM."
- **Failover paths written** — pre-recorded demo if live product fails, backup walk-on music, manual scenic reset if automation hangs.

**Show day:**

- **Pre-show checklist signed** — every department confirms ready state before doors; not a verbal "we're good."
- **Timed run at least once** — full cue-to-cue at show pace, including presenter pauses and audience reaction time.
- **Post-rehearsal change control** — any run-of-show edit after final rehearsal requires show caller and department head sign-off.

Planners who miss the four-week content lock are the ones requesting a new sizzle reel at 6 AM. Production can often accommodate it. They cannot accommodate discovering it doesn't match the wall resolution at 7:45.

## Questions to bring your production vendor

A product launch brief that only lists "LED wall, 4 cameras, wireless mics" invites guesswork on the reveal — the moment that actually matters. Bring these to your first production meeting:

- **Who owns the run-of-show document** — client marketing, production company, or shared; and who has final authority on cue changes during rehearsal.
- **Show caller assignment** — dedicated show caller or shared with stage manager; confirm they have the final run-of-show and comms to every department.
- **Automation vs. manual cues** — which transitions run on timecode or QLab, and which depend on a stagehand or presenter trigger.
- **Reveal sync tolerance** — how tight must scenic, video, lighting, and audio land; sub-second, or is a half-beat drift acceptable on a live button press.
- **IMAG and stream paths** — separate switcher presets for in-room IMAG vs. broadcast feed; confirm the reveal reads on camera, not just to the live audience.
- **Content testing protocol** — when and where full-resolution content plays on the actual wall, not a preview monitor.
- **Rehearsal attendance requirements** — which segments need the CEO on stage vs. a stand-in; product demo segments almost always need the actual operator.
- **Change window cutoff** — last time a run-of-show edit can be made without overtime or missed cues.

Answers belong in writing. Verbal agreements at the production meeting evaporate when three departments interpret "after the reveal" differently.

## Rehearsal gates — test the show, not the gear

Gear checks confirm inputs work. Rehearsal gates confirm the show works — the reveal timing, the presenter pacing, the failover nobody wants to use. Block rehearsal time against these gates, not against a vague "tech run."

Rehearsal sequence for a product launch:

1. **Segment isolation** — run the reveal three times cold: no warm-up, no "we'll get it in the full run." If the reveal fails in isolation, it fails in the show.
2. **Full cue-to-cue at pace** — presenters at actual speed, including pauses for applause and demo setup; timecode-only rehearsals lie about show length.
3. **Fail one path** — kill the primary media server output, drop the automation trigger, or mute the presenter lav; confirm backup procedure and who calls it.
4. **Camera and IMAG review** — watch the switcher output on a confidence monitor sized like your IMAG wall; a reveal that looks dramatic live can read as a dark blob on the back row screens.
5. **Audio handoffs** — walk-on music into VOG into presenter lav into demo audio; confirm ducking, echo, and that demo audio routes to both house and stream.
6. **Reset drill** — time how long scenic and stage take to return to pre-show state; if it exceeds your transition window, the run-of-show needs a hold or a crew add.

Document who calls what: show caller owns cue execution, technical director owns switcher and playback, audio lead owns mic state and playback levels, lighting lead owns preset transitions. Product launches add roles; they don't add time unless the run-of-show accounts for it.

## From the floor: the reveal that waited for a file name

One launch rehearsal hit the reveal cue perfectly — scrim up, lighting snap, audio sting — and then thirty seconds of holding slide while the media server searched for a file that had been renamed that morning. The run-of-show listed "Demo_Reveal_Final_v3"; the server held "Demo_Reveal_Final_v3_APPROVED."

Same content. Different string. The show caller held the room while someone typed.

The fix was a content naming convention in the run-of-show itself — exact file names, verified in the pre-show checklist, no aliases. The reveal cue didn't change. The thirty seconds of stillness did.

Product launch AV is not about owning every fader move. It's about owning the milestones — content lock, cue documentation, rehearsal gates, and the sign-offs that keep departments reading the same document on show day.
