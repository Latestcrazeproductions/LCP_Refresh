Production proposals are written for people who already know what a line array is. They are not written for the marketing director who has to defend the PO to finance or the events lead who needs to know whether rehearsal survived the union dock schedule. You do not need to become an AV engineer to read a bid — you need a reading order that surfaces scope, assumptions, and business risk before you compare totals. This guide gives you that order: what to scan first, which line items actually move outcomes, and the gaps that turn a "competitive" bid into a show-day change order.

## Read the proposal top-down before you touch the gear list

Most reviewers start at page three with microphones and truss. That is backwards. The first pass should answer three executive questions: what outcome this scope supports, who owns show day, and what happens when the script slips. If those answers are missing, the gear list is a catalog — not a plan.

Use this reading order on every bid. Fifteen minutes per proposal, same sequence every time.

1. **Scope narrative and assumptions** — Does the vendor restate your brief in plain language? Are assumptions flagged — house power, venue rigging, content provided by client, rehearsal window tied to dock access? Unwritten assumptions become invoices.
2. **Labor roles and hours** — Who is on site for load-in, rehearsal, show, and strike? Is there a named show caller or technical director, or is labor bundled as "production crew"?
3. **Rehearsal and advance allocation** — Is rehearsal time in the bid or billed hourly if load-in runs long? Advance hours for RF survey, sight-line review, and stream path testing should appear before show week — not as implied prep.
4. **Flagship segment coverage** — Which segments get redundancy, dedicated switching, and capture routing? The CEO keynote and product reveal should be named, not buried under "video package."
5. **Deliverables and handoff** — What files, formats, and timelines are committed? "We'll share footage" is not a deliverable. Marketing needs program audio, capture scope, and turnaround in writing.
6. **Change order triggers** — What requires a signed change order versus what the vendor absorbs? Scope without a change process becomes a relationship problem at awards segment.

Only after that sequence should you review equipment line items. Gear without narrative tells you what they rent — not what they protect.

## Line item categories that actually move business outcomes

Proposals organize costs differently. One vendor bundles labor; another itemizes every cable. Compare categories, not row count. These buckets map to outcomes executives care about — message fidelity, schedule integrity, content leverage, and show-day risk.

**Production management and show calling** — The line item that separates a partner from a gear house. Show calling, client communication, run-of-show discipline, and escalation authority belong here — not under "miscellaneous labor." If this category is thin or absent, someone else is calling your show by default — usually whoever got to FOH first.

**Technical direction and engineering** — RF coordination, power load calculations, signal path design, and backup routing. Thin engineering on a hybrid or multi-camera program is a reliability bet you did not knowingly make.

**Audio** — House mix, program bus for stream and capture, wireless inventory and spare count, monitor world for presenters. Program audio routed only to the room — with no isolated bus for post-production — is a marketing problem that surfaces in the edit bay.

**Video and IMAG** — Switching, playback, camera crew, LED or projection processing, delay screens. Ask whether switching hours include rehearsal for CEO walk-on and demo segments — not just show runtime.

**Lighting** — Key light for faces on IMAG and camera, not just wash. Flat overhead that reads fine to the eye often kills executive presence on stream and in recordings.

**Scenic and rigging** — Stage deck, draping, ground support versus flown structures. Rigging line items should reference venue constraints and who pulls power — not just tonnage.

**Labor by phase** — Load-in, rehearsal, show, strike — with day rates or hour blocks explicit. Bids that only show "show day" often hid load-in overtime until the invoice.

**Travel, per diem, and local crew** — For multi-market programs, who travels versus who is hired locally? Inconsistent crew across cities becomes a brand consistency problem marketing inherits.

When two bids differ by twenty percent, the gap is usually in production management, rehearsal, program audio routing, and backup paths — not in whether one quoted two more HDMI cables.

## Red flags and silent gaps to catch before signature

Identical-looking totals hide different assumptions. These patterns predict show-day surprises and post-event fights about what was "included."

- **Gear pages without narrative** — Equipment lists with no explanation of how choices serve sight lines, capture, or flagship segments. Pages of SKUs are not a production plan.
- **"Included as needed"** — Show calling, rehearsal, backup switching, and spare wireless should be quantified. Vague inclusion language is how scope disappears.
- **No named roles** — "Our team will be on site" without who runs the show, who owns client comms, and who has authority to hold or skip segments.
- **Rehearsal as optional or hourly** — Rehearsal compressed by dock schedules is the most common failure mode on high-stakes keynotes. If rehearsal is not locked in the bid, it will be the first line cut under load-in pressure.
- **Single-path video and audio** — No hot backup on the segment that cannot fail. Redundancy omitted from the proposal is redundancy sold as a change order on show week.
- **Capture scope undefined** — Which segments are multi-camera record-ready, who approves framing, and whether program audio is isolated. Undefined capture scope produces footage marketing cannot use — at full production cost.
- **No assumption appendix** — Best vendors flag what they expect you to provide: content, power, rigging points, union rules, internet for stream. Bids with zero assumptions are bids with hidden ones.

Flag these in writing during vendor Q&A. The response quality tells you as much as the original bid.

## How to compare proposals without a spreadsheet war

Finance will ask for a side-by-side. Give them one — but normalize scope before you normalize price. A cheap bid that omits show calling, rehearsal, and program audio is not cheaper; it is incomplete.

Build a comparison grid with the same rows for every vendor:

| Row | What to compare |
| --- | --- |
| Outcome restatement | Did they paraphrase your business objective accurately — launch credibility, partner confidence, content reuse — not just room dimensions? |
| Show-day authority | Named show caller or TD with escalation path to client? |
| Rehearsal hours | Fixed allocation by phase, or hourly with no floor? |
| Flagship redundancy | Specific backup paths on named segments — power, signal, wireless, switching? |
| Capture and deliverables | Segments covered, program audio routing, file format, turnaround timeline? |
| Change order process | Written triggers and approval path before show week? |
| Assumptions | Client-provided items, venue dependencies, union or dock constraints flagged? |

Weight outcome alignment and scope transparency above price alone. Procurement can compare totals after marketing and events confirm the rows are apples to apples. For a question set to use during vendor evaluation before proposals arrive, see our guide to [production RFP questions for marketing leaders](/blog/production-rfp-questions-for-marketing-leaders).

Share the grid internally before finalist presentations. When three stakeholders walk in with three different definitions of "included," the vendor who answered most clearly to all three usually earns the repeat booking — not the one who won on line-item density.

## Business outcomes to verify before you sign

The signed proposal is the contract narrative finance and leadership will reference when something goes wrong on stream or in the recording. These outcome checks belong in your final review — not as gear questions, but as accountability questions.

Does the scope protect message fidelity on your highest-stakes segments — farthest-seat legibility, IMAG timing, presenter confidence monitors, and stream sync? Does it protect schedule integrity with named authority to hold or cut when the CEO runs long? Does it protect content leverage with capture scope, clean program audio, and committed deliverables marketing can actually publish? Does it protect reliability with redundancy and rehearsal time on the segments where visible failure becomes a brand problem?

If any answer is "we'll confirm at load-in," treat that line as unsigned scope. Load-in is for execution against a locked plan — not for discovering what the bid forgot.

Partners who think in outcomes put show calling, rehearsal, capture routing, and assumption flags in the proposal because they know that is what you are buying. Partners who think in gear put microphones on page four and hope you do not read page one twice.

## From the floor: when the totals matched and the scope did not

Two proposals landed within six percent of each other — same ballroom, same screen size, same camera count on the cover sheet. Finance asked why marketing wanted the higher number. Marketing opened the labor section. The lower bid bundled "production management" into four hours on show day. The higher bid named a show caller for rehearsal through strike, with twelve hours of advance documented in the scope narrative.

Nobody compared rehearsal rows because both bids listed identical wireless inventory. Rehearsal was compressed to ninety minutes when the dock schedule the lower vendor never flagged ate the morning. The CEO segment ran long; the stream showed the previous slide for eleven seconds while someone found the right laptop. Marketing got one usable wide shot and program audio that sounded like the room — because nobody routed a bus for post. The savings against the higher bid was about $11,000. The reshoot quote lived on a different tab, which is where AV savings always look best.

Read top-down. Compare categories. Sign scope — not SKU count.
