Trade show and festival activations fail in the paperwork gap between the render and the floor plan. You sign a booth package with a footprint on paper, then discover the LED column needs six inches you do not have, the house circuit trips when the demo rig powers up, and freight cannot reach your aisle without a union hand-cart fee nobody quoted. This guide covers the footprint and power decisions to document before you sign the booth agreement — so production can advance against real numbers instead of a sales packet.

## Footprint: what to measure beyond length and width

Organizers sell booths in standard sizes. Production lives in the three-dimensional envelope — height, depth of structure, audience circulation, and the path cases take from dock to carpet.

**Footprint checklist before you commit:**

1. **Usable floor area** — Deduct column wraps, fire-equipment offsets, and organizer-mandated buffer zones from the labeled size. A 20×20 contract often yields less buildable deck once corner radials and aisle setbacks apply.
2. **Maximum build height** — Ceiling hang height, top of scenic, and whether double-deck or hanging elements are allowed. Fine-pitch LED at eye level may need a taller totem than the package permits.
3. **Depth for guest flow** — Minimum aisle width for queue, demo stations, and staff egress. A wall that eats four feet of depth leaves no room for a six-foot interaction table and a camera cross-shoot.
4. **Overhead obstructions** — Sprinklers, house lighting, rigging grid, and venue HVAC drops that limit truss or fabric headers. Mark them on your plan; do not discover them when the scenic is already built.
5. **Adjacent booth overlap** — Shared walls, sight-line conflicts, and noise bleed from a neighbor’s subwoofer. Contract language should name who owns the shared boundary and what can project into your zone.
6. **Strike and storage** — Whether empty cases can live in-booth overnight, and whether overnight power is billed separately. Footprint math includes the pile of road cases that otherwise block the demo floor.

Bring a dimensioned plan to the organizer review — not a brand deck. If the structure does not fit in the envelope with crew access, the envelope is wrong, not the creative.

## Power plan: amps, circuits, and who owns the distro

“Power included” on an exhibitor order form rarely means production-grade distribution. It usually means one or two Edison circuits at the back curtain, shared with the booth behind you, rated for carpet lights — not a media server, LED processor rack, and a charging bank for forty demo devices.

Treat power like rigging: name locations, amperage, and responsibility before deposit.

**Minimum power spec to put in writing:**

- **Circuit count and amperage** — Separate circuits for LED/video, audio, and lighting when combined load exceeds 20 amps per leg. Ask for 120V and 208V availability if touring gear includes both.
- **Tie-in location** — Booth number is not a location. Specify left rear, center rear, or organizer-provided floor ports with panel IDs. Production needs a home run path that does not cross guest traffic.
- **Dedicated vs. shared** — Require confirmation that AV loads are not on the same breaker as carpet warmers, HVAC taps, or the coffee station next door.
- **Distro ownership** — Who supplies cam-lock or Edison distro from the tie-in to your positions: show electrician, your production partner, or the official contractor? Name it and cap hourly rates if show labor is mandatory.
- **Peak load estimate** — Sum LED walls, lighting, demo PCs, chargers, and refrigeration if you run cold product. Add 20% headroom so the first full power-up does not darken the aisle.
- **Grounding and cable routing** — Where cables may run (under carpet, overhead, in drape pockets) and whether cable covers are organizer-supplied or your responsibility. Tripping an attendee is a brand moment you do not want.

For outdoor activations and street festivals, add generator sizing, fuel curfew, and noise ordinances to the same document. House power and generator power should not be “we’ll figure it out on load-in day.”

## Technology stack: how footprint and power interact

The same activation can be a 10-foot kiosk or a 40-foot immersive tunnel. Footprint drives structure; structure drives draw. Lock the experience stack early so power and floor load stay honest in the contract.

**Common load and space drivers:**

| Element | Footprint impact | Power note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fine-pitch LED hero wall | Depth for structure and viewing distance | High sustained draw; often needs dedicated circuit |
| Interactive demo stations | Table depth plus queue lane | PCs, displays, and USB charging add quiet load that adds up |
| Moving light or pixel strip scenic | Height and rigging allowance | Dimming and inrush; avoid sharing with video processors |
| Audio for ambient or presentations | Speaker placement and sight lines | Subs on the deck may need floor load confirmation |
| Photo or stream capture | Camera positions and cable paths | Encoder table needs stable power away from dimmer noise |

If the creative calls for a last-minute “small LED strip along the header,” rerun the power one-line. Strip counts are how 20-amp circuits die quietly at 7 a.m.

Align with your production partner on a single advance deliverable: dimensioned booth plot, power one-line, and equipment list with nameplate draws. That packet is what you attach to the exhibitor agreement — not a paragraph that says “standard AV power.”

## Questions for the show organizer before you sign

Require written answers in the contract or technical addendum. Email from the sales rep is not load-in documentation.

1. **What is the exact usable build dimension, and what is the maximum height including hanging elements?**
2. **How many dedicated circuits serve the booth, what amperage, and where are the outlets or floor ports?**
3. **Are circuits shared with adjacent booths or house loads, and can show engineering confirm in writing?**
4. **Who provides distro from the tie-in to our rack positions, and what are labor rates and call rules?**
5. **What are floor load limits for stage deck, LED ground support, or heavy product displays?**
6. **What are load-in and strike windows, freight routing, and fees for early access or overtime electrician?**
7. **What is the policy on generators, fuel storage, and overnight power if we run demos before hall open?**

Organizers who answer with a floor plan and panel schedule belong on the short list. Organizers who answer with “everyone makes it work” belong on a call with your production partner before anyone signs.

## From the floor: ten feet on paper, nine feet six in the carpet

A consumer tech brand signed a premium island booth labeled 10×20. Creative called for a ground-supported LED totem and a demo bar across the front. Load-in measured the carpet: nine feet six at the narrow point because the organizer had placed a fire-equipment offset that did not appear on the sales drawing.

The totem structure could not rotate without overlapping the aisle. Production shaved scenic depth and dropped a side panel from the LED — the hero animation still ran, but the framing the agency sold in the render did not. Power was a single 20-amp circuit at the back left. The first simultaneous power-up of the wall, four demo laptops, and the lighting rack tripped the breaker twice before show electrician installed a second leg — billed hourly, not included in the package.

The following year’s agreement listed usable dimensions with a dimensioned plot, two dedicated 20-amp circuits with panel IDs, floor load confirmation for the deck, and load-in exclusivity for the hour before the hall opened. Same show. Same aisle. Different paperwork — and a build that matched the brief the first time the tools came out of the cases.

Footprint and power are not details for the production vendor to “handle later.” They are booth infrastructure questions that belong in your agreement before the signature line.
