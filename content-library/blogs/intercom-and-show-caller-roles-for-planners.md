Intercom discipline is what keeps a corporate show from sounding like a airport gate on show morning. The show caller cannot run cue-to-cue if stage managers, video shade, and client-side producers share one channel — or if nobody in writing owns the run-of-show.

This guide covers what planners should assign before cases hit the dock: named show-caller authority, a channel map every department signs off on, belt-pack roles that match your format, and the vendor questions that prevent "everyone on channel 1" from becoming your comms plan.

## Show caller vs technical director — split the job on paper

The show caller executes the run-of-show: segment timing, holds, presenter handoffs, and the verbal cues that tie audio, video, and lighting together. The technical director owns switcher, playback, and the technical path behind those cues. When one person does both on a complex general session, something always slips — usually during a panel turnover while they are troubleshooting a playback codec.

Before load-in, document:

- **Named show caller** — one person, one shift (or a documented handoff if the role splits across rehearsal and show). Not "the production company." Not the account manager unless they are trained and contracted for calling.
- **Named technical director** — owns switcher, server, and failover; responds to the show caller on defined cues, not parallel direction from the client table.
- **Authority to hold or skip** — show caller can extend Q&A, compress B-roll, or call a hard out without a committee; client approver is identified for content holds, not timing micro-management.
- **Run-of-show version** — show caller works from vFinal with sub-cues; if marketing edits after rehearsal, the document version bumps and department heads re-confirm.

Ambiguity here costs more than a dedicated caller line item. It costs the ninety-second panel turnover that never got rehearsed because three people thought someone else was watching the clock.

## Intercom tiers — who belongs on which channel

Corporate programs need at least two production channels before you add talent IFB, door security, or a separate stream team. More channels beat more people on fewer channels — every time.

**Typical channel map for a flagship general session or awards block:**

| Channel | Who listens | Who talks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Show / calling** | Show caller, stage managers (both wings), A2, video shade, lighting lead on show cues | Show caller primary; stage managers for handoffs and "talent at mark" |
| **Tech / coordination** | TD, video director, shader, playback op, RF tech | TD primary; department heads for fixes, not show cues |
| **Talent IFB / confidence** | Presenters on IFB or one-way feed | Show caller or dedicated VOG operator only — no client walk-through audio on presenter ears |
| **Security / doors (optional)** | Venue security, door captain, registration lead | Separate from calling; no show cues on this channel |

Rules to put in the production brief:

- **Client and marketing stay off the calling channel** — they get a producer liaison on tech or a dedicated "client" channel if the budget supports it; never the channel where "stand by on walk-on" lives.
- **Wings report to show caller, not to FOH** — A1 mix notes do not belong on the calling channel during live segments.
- **Radio check before every cue-to-cue** — belt packs labeled with role, not just name; spare packs charged and assigned before load-in day two.
- **IFB isolation** — presenter IFB and show comms on separate systems or bands; bleed is how a stage manager's "two minutes" ends up in the CEO's ear during the walk-on.

If your format adds breakouts, overflow rooms, or a hybrid stream team, add channels rather than stacking roles. A stream engineer troubleshooting encoder delay on the same channel as the show caller will lose every time.

## Belt-pack roles to assign before load-in

Headsets are not courtesy gear for anyone with a laminate. Assign packs by function and publish the list on the call sheet, the callboard, and the brief — same three places, same names.

**Minimum assignments for a multi-segment corporate show:**

1. **Show caller** — calling position with sight line to stage and monitor for run-of-show; not from the audience seating area unless that is the only FOH option and everyone agrees in writing.
2. **Stage manager stage left and stage right** — one per active wing during turnover-heavy segments; floaters miss handoffs.
3. **A2 or audio lead** — on calling channel for mic state, mute discipline, and playback handoffs; not running monitor world on a different channel without a relay plan.
4. **Video shade or TD** — on calling for "take slide," "roll video," and IMAG switches tied to show cues; tech fixes move to the tech channel.
5. **Lighting lead** — on calling for preset transitions tied to segments; follow-spot callers on their own channel if spots are manual and chatty.
6. **Producer liaison (client side or vendor)** — off calling; filters client requests into the show caller through one path.

Scale up for awards rapid turnover (extra wing stage manager), product demos (playback op on calling with TD on tech), or multi-room overflow (door captains on security channel only). Scale down only when the run-of-show is a single keynote with one exit — and even then, name who calls "mic live."

Questions for your production partner before the channel map is final:

- **Belt-pack count and spare ratio** — how many packs in the quote vs. roles on your call sheet; who pays for extras when marketing adds a second green room wrangler.
- **Clear-Com / intercom topology** — party-line vs. matrix; whether channels are fixed in hardware or re-patchable mid-show (re-patching mid-show is a last resort, not a plan).
- **Show-caller station** — desk, monitor, run-of-show hard copy, and whether the caller can see both stage and playback confirmation without turning away during a live cue.
- **Rehearsal comms drill** — full radio check, simulated dead pack swap, and a turnover run with only calling-channel cues (no texting fixes from the client table).
- **Overtime and handoff** — if show caller shifts at dinner break, documented handoff time and which run-of-show version the incoming caller confirms.

Answers belong in the production brief distributed to venue security, talent wranglers, and client-side producers — not in a sidebar at the pre-pro meeting.

## When to add a second caller or matrix channel

Single show caller fits many hotel ballrooms. Add structure when complexity crosses these lines:

- **Simultaneous stages or overflow** — main session plus hard-feed overflow needs a door captain and often a second caller or a dedicated stream director with their own channel map.
- **Awards with sub-sixty-second turnover** — dedicated wing stage managers plus a caller who is not also reading sponsor copy changes from the client.
- **Hybrid with live remote inserts** — remote Q&A, live polls, or CEO dial-in adds latency decisions the caller must own; stream team on separate comms, synced in rehearsal with documented delay.
- **Executive protection or high-security formats** — security channel stays isolated; show caller does not become the routing hub for VIP movement and show cues.

Under-staffing comms shows up as whispered fixes from the front row, texts to the TD during the walk-on, and presenters who start early because nobody confirmed "stand by" on IFB. Over-staffing without a map shows up as four people saying "go" at once. The map is the product.

## From the floor: channel one at cue-to-cue

One partner summit loaded intercom for a general session plus an evening awards block. Pre-pro agreed on "show channel and tech channel." Load-in day, the client's marketing lead picked up a spare pack "to listen in." By rehearsal, channel one had the show caller, both stage managers, the TD, video shade, lighting, two wranglers, and marketing — because nobody wanted to miss the CEO block.

Cue-to-cue for the panel turnover: the TD asked for a playback fix, a wrangler called "talent moving," and marketing said "can we hold for photos?" in the same three seconds. The show caller missed the A2's "mic two muted" call. The first presenter walked on with a dead lav. Recovery took forty seconds that were not in the run-of-show.

The fix was not a new intercom system. It was a printed channel map, packs labeled by role, marketing on a client liaison channel, and a rule that only the show caller says "go" on the calling channel. The next turnover drill ran clean. Channel one sounded like a show again — which is to say, almost silent until someone needed it.

Intercom and show-caller assignments are not AV minutiae. They are org chart decisions: who owns timing, who owns the switcher, and which voices reach the people on stage. Lock names and channels before load-in, rehearse comms under show pace, and keep client curiosity off the calling channel.
