Walk-in look is the lighting, wall content, and house-light state attendees see while they find seats — before anyone on stage earns their attention. Get it wrong and the first five minutes feel like a tech hold; get it right and the room reads “we’re starting” without a word from the emcee. This guide covers what to name in your brief, how walk-in differs from your session look, and what to lock with lighting, video, and show caller before doors.

Walk-in is not “house at fifty percent.” It is a named preset with owners, levels, and a defined handoff into your opening cue stack. If your [general session design](/blog/general-session-design-tips) already names session, video, and Q&A looks, walk-in is the preset that must exist before those — and the one teams skip in rehearsal most often.

## What walk-in has to accomplish

Attendees are scanning for seats, reading the program, and judging production quality from row twenty before your host speaks. Walk-in look should signal brand, orient people to the stage, and keep the first rows comfortable — without blinding anyone or fighting signage on the wall.

Use this checklist when you review the plot or run-of-show:

- **Safe navigation** — enough house light for aisles and steps; no followspot or blinders hitting seated faces during load-in music.
- **Stage presence** — a readable wash on set pieces and lectern so the room has a focal point; empty stage should not read as “power failure.”
- **Wall or IMAG content** — loop, holding slide, or subtle motion that matches brand; no accidental full-white frames that flash the front half of the room.
- **Audio bed** — music or ambient at a level that allows conversation in the back; confirm who ducks or mutes for the open.
- **Clock alignment** — walk-in ends on a schedule tied to your [opening pacing](/blog/general-session-design-tips), not when the CEO arrives at the green room.

Walk-in also sets camera shading if you are streaming. LD and video shader should agree on exposure during focus — not when doors are already open.

## Name the preset — levels, wall, and handoff

Vague brief language (“warm and welcoming”) produces a different look at every venue. Treat walk-in like any other cue: stored on the console, labeled in the run-of-show, and owned by a single operator role.

Lock these fields in your production brief:

- **House dimmer** — target percentage or foot-candles for note-taking vs. theater feel; who holds the master (ballroom staff vs. production).
- **Stage wash** — fixture groups, color temperature, and intensity on lectern, set, and any visible panel chairs; no unlit “dead zone” where the emcee will stand.
- **Specials off or parked** — followspots, strobes, and audience blinders disabled or in a safe state until session look.
- **Wall / media state** — loop file name, holding slide ID, or “wall dim” for ambient-only walk-in; failover slide if playback fails.
- **Handoff cue** — the exact call that moves from walk-in to “we are starting” (e.g. emcee line, timecode, or show-caller “session 1”); includes house fade, wall change, and first mic open.
- **Duration policy** — minimum walk-in window, hard stop before downbeat, and whether you extend if seating is slow.

If you already use named looks for session and video, walk-in should appear on the same list as the first row — not as a footnote under “pre-show.” [Keynote stage lighting](/blog/keynote-stage-lighting-for-planners) presets apply here; walk-in is often the missing label between load-in focus and the first spoken cue.

## Right-size the rig — when to add or trim

Walk-in does not need a Super Bowl rig, but underspecing shows the moment the house drops for the open. Scale for room size, camera count, and how many distinct looks you run in one session block.

**Lean (single-room general session, no IMAG, house stays 40%+):**

- Basic stage wash and lectern light, one console preset for walk-in and session
- Static holding slide or simple loop on one display path
- House controlled by venue with production advising minimum levels

**Standard (corporate general session with IMAG or stream):**

- Color-matched LED wash, separate walk-in and session presets on the desk
- Wall loop reviewed for legibility and average picture level — no full-frame white between slides
- LD rehearsal time for handoff: walk-in → emcee → first slide or first camera shot

**Heavy (multi-segment day, awards or panels in the same room):**

- Distinct walk-in looks if room resets between segments (lunch return, evening gala in same ballroom)
- Media server cues tied to lighting presets; show caller sheet lists preset names, not “vibes”
- Backup path if loop fails — holding slide on a switcher input, not a laptop hunt

When budget tightens, cut moving lights before you cut a defined walk-in preset. Attendees notice a sloppy open more than they notice missing beam effects during a loop.

## Questions to bring your lighting vendor

Walk-in arguments usually surface at load-in when house, wall, and music are owned by three different people. Ask these before the truck rolls:

1. **What is the stored preset name for walk-in, and who executes the handoff?** — Confirm it matches the run-of-show labels show caller will use.
2. **What house level do you need for safe seating, and who controls the master dimmer?** — Get it in writing if ballroom staff retain control.
3. **How does walk-in exposure match your first session look on camera?** — Avoid a visible jump when IMAG or stream goes live.
4. **What happens on the wall if playback crashes during walk-in?** — Failover slide, freeze last frame, or cut to dimmer — pick one.
5. **How much rehearsal time is allocated for the open sequence only?** — Walk-in through first slide or first line, in order, at least twice.

## From the floor: the handoff nobody ran

Doors opened on a clean walk-in — brand wash, loop on the wall, house at a sensible forty percent. The emcee walked to the mark early, waved at someone in the front row, and the LD interpreted that as “go to show.” House crashed to ten percent, the loop cut to black, and the wall sat empty for eight seconds while the emcee ad-libbed welcome lines in a room that suddenly felt like a cave. The sequence was fine on paper. It had never been run with a human on the mark before the scheduled open.

The fix was boring and effective: a show-caller-only handoff on a single word, walk-in preset left active until that call, and three minutes in rehearsal for “emcee on mark but not yet show” — which, it turns out, happens in every general session. Production quality is often just presets with names and a rehearsal for the transition everyone assumes will happen naturally.

Walk-in look design is the first impression your general session makes in the room. Name the preset, align house and wall with your open, right-size the package for your format, and rehearse the handoff like it is a cue — because it is.
